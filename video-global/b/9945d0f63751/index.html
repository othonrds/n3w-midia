Chapter 1 — Your AI studio

Which tool for which job, what's free, what's worth paying for, and how to set up a studio that fits in a browser tab.

What you'll make

  • A side-by-side "bake-off" of the same image prompt in two free image tools, so you see each tool's personality with your own eyes
  • Your first 5-second clip made from a still image
  • A 20-second voiceover with real emotion in it (whispers, a laugh, a pause)
  • A one-page tool cost map you can print and stick next to your screen
  • A starter stack picked for your budget: zero, one subscription, or full studio

Hi, I'm Maggy. I'm AI-generated myself, so I know every tell, and I know how many tools exist to make things like me.

Most people get plastic-looking results because they picked the wrong tool for the job. Then they blame the prompt. This chapter fixes that first, so the rest of the book works.

The one rule: tools change, jobs don't

New models drop almost every month. Names change, versions jump, prices move. If you learn tools by name, you'll be relearning forever.

So learn the jobs instead. Every AI photo and video studio, whether it costs nothing or a few hundred dollars a year, does the same six jobs:

  1. Make an image (image generator)
  2. Move an image (video generator)
  3. Hold everything in one place (all-in-one hub)
  4. Cut, caption and export (editor)
  5. Sharpen and enlarge (upscaler)
  6. Give it a voice and a sound (voice and audio)

When a shiny new tool shows up, ask one question: which job does it do, and does it do it better than what I have? If you can't answer, you don't need it yet.

Heads up: Everything named in this chapter was checked as of late 2026. Tools get renamed, merged and shut down. In 2026 OpenAI announced it was discontinuing its Sora video app and API, which had been one of the best-known video tools. That's exactly why this book teaches principles first and tools second.

Job 1: Image generators

An image generator is your camera, your studio and your set designer. Every photo in Part 2 of this book starts here, and every video in Part 3 starts with a still from here.

Gemini app (Google's Nano Banana models)

Google's image models go by the nickname Nano Banana. As of late 2026, Nano Banana 2 is the default image model inside the Gemini app, and paid subscribers can switch a result to Nano Banana Pro.

Best for: editing photos with plain-language instructions, keeping the same person or product across several images, and readable text inside images. Google says it can keep the likeness of up to five characters and the form of up to 14 objects in one workflow.

Free? Yes, you can generate images in the free Gemini app. Limits vary, so check the app.

Pro tip: Gemini is the tool I reach for when I need to change a photo rather than invent one. "Keep everything, swap the background for a rainy street" is its home turf.

ChatGPT Images

OpenAI's image generation lives inside ChatGPT. As of late 2026 it runs on a model OpenAI calls ChatGPT Images 2.0 (gpt-image-2 for developers). Standard generation is available to free users; a "thinking" mode that plans the image and can search the web is for paid plans.

Best for: layouts with text (posters, menus, infographics), following long, detailed instructions, and making several consistent variations in one go.

Free? Yes, with limits. Paid plans unlock the thinking mode.

Midjourney

Midjourney is the aesthetic one: rich color, beautiful light, painterly polish out of the box. A gift for mood boards, a trap for realism if you don't steer it.

As of late 2026, its default model is in the V8 family, and it has a separate Niji line for anime and illustration styles. It can also animate a still into a short clip.

Free? No. Midjourney's own help pages say no free trial is available on its website or Discord. The cheapest plan is Basic (check current pricing).

Flux (Black Forest Labs)

Flux is a family of image models from Black Forest Labs. You usually don't visit "Flux" directly. You use it inside other apps and hubs, or through its API.

The Flux 2 family covers high-quality generation and editing in one model, precise color control by hex code, and support for JSON-style prompts (you'll learn those in Chapter 2). Some smaller Flux versions are open weights, which means tech-savvy users can run them on their own computer.

Free? Depends on where you use it. Some versions are open, but check each license before commercial use. Paid use is usually per image through an app or API (check site).

The image bake-off

Don't trust reviews, including mine. Run the same prompt in two free tools and compare side by side.

Use this portrait prompt first:

Prompt
A candid photo of a [AGE]-year-old [WOMAN/MAN] who runs a small ceramics studio, standing at a pottery wheel with clay on [HER/HIS] hands, laughing at something off camera. Late afternoon window light from the left, dust in the air, shelves of unglazed mugs behind. Shot on a 50mm lens at eye level, shallow depth of field, natural skin texture with visible pores, slight film grain. Vertical 4:5.

Then a product prompt, because products expose a different set of weaknesses (labels, reflections, edges):

Prompt
Product photo of a matte [COLOR] ceramic coffee mug with a small chip on the rim, on a weathered oak table next to a folded linen napkin and three coffee beans. Soft morning light from a window behind, gentle shadow falling toward the camera. Shot from a 30-degree angle with a 100mm macro lens, background softly blurred, realistic reflections, no text, no logo. Square 1:1.

Then test text, the classic AI weak spot:

Prompt
A hand-painted chalkboard sign leaning against a café doorway on a sunny sidewalk. The sign reads exactly: "[YOUR 3-5 WORD MESSAGE]" in white chalk capital letters, with a small doodle of a [OBJECT] underneath. Slight chalk smudges, morning shadows, shot on a phone at waist height. Vertical 9:16.

Score each result from 1 to 5 on three things: skin or surface texture, light that makes sense, and details that hold up when you zoom in (fingers, rims, letters). Keep the winner's name. That's your default image tool for now.

Editing by instruction

The biggest change of the last two years: you don't need to regenerate from scratch. You upload a photo and tell the tool what to change. Gemini, ChatGPT, Flux and Midjourney's newer editing tools all do this in some form.

Prompt
Using the photo I uploaded, keep the person, their face, pose, clothing and expression exactly the same. Replace only the background with [NEW SETTING, e.g. "a quiet bookshop with warm lamps"]. Match the new background's light direction and color temperature to the light already on their face. Keep the original camera angle and lens look. Do not change their skin, hair or body shape.

Fix it: If the face changes during an edit, say what must stay first in the prompt and what changes second. Models pay more attention to the start.

Job 2: Video generators

A video generator turns either a description (text-to-video) or a still image (image-to-video) into a short clip, usually somewhere between 4 and 15 seconds.

Here's the most useful thing I can tell you about video: start from an image you already love. Image-to-video gives you control over the face, the outfit, the light and the framing. Text-to-video makes all those decisions for you, and they're rarely the decisions you'd make.

Google: Flow, Veo and Gemini Omni

Google has two video model lines as of late 2026:

  • Veo (the 3.1 family, including Lite, Fast and Quality versions), known for high-fidelity clips with generated sound.
  • Gemini Omni, announced at Google I/O in May 2026, a model you can talk to like a chat to create and edit video from text, images, audio and video.

You use them through Flow (Google's filmmaking app), the Gemini app, and, for Omni's fast version, inside YouTube Shorts and the YouTube Create app at no cost.

Free? As of late 2026, Flow offers a small free daily credit allowance that works on some Veo versions. Gemini Omni in the Gemini app and Flow requires a Google AI plan (Plus, Pro or Ultra). Check current terms, because Google has changed them several times this year.

Kling

Kling is a favorite for human motion: walking, dancing, turning, hands doing things. As of late 2026 its newest line is Kling 3 (in Standard and Pro modes) with sound on by default, and an older 2.6 line offers Motion Control, where you copy the movement from a reference video onto your character.

Free? Yes, a small daily credit allowance, with a watermark and no commercial use on the free tier. Paid plans start at Standard (check current pricing).

Runway

Runway is a full creative suite with its own video models. Its Gen-4.5 model, released at the end of 2025, is known for realistic physics: weight, momentum, fabric and hair that move like they should.

Free? A one-time set of starter credits with a watermark. Gen-4.5 is on paid plans (Standard and up, check current pricing).

Seedance (ByteDance)

Seedance 2.0 is ByteDance's video model. It accepts a mix of inputs at once (up to 9 images, 3 video clips and 3 audio clips), can cut between several shots inside one clip, and generates synced dialogue, ambience and music.

You'll find it inside CapCut (paid tier, in many countries), on Dreamina (ByteDance's web app), and inside hubs like Higgsfield.

Heads up: CapCut blocks uploads that contain real faces or copyrighted characters when generating with Seedance. That's a feature, not a bug. It keeps you on the right side of Chapter 3's consent rules.

Midjourney video

If you already pay for Midjourney, you can animate any of your stills into a short clip (image-to-video only, no text-to-video). It's lovely for moody, atmospheric motion and less precise for acting.

Your first motion prompt

A motion prompt is different from an image prompt. The image already shows what is there. Your job now is to say what moves, how, and what the camera does.

Prompt
Image-to-video, 5 seconds. Use the uploaded photo as the first frame. The woman slowly looks up from the pottery wheel toward the window and smiles softly; her hands stay on the clay. Dust drifts in the light beam. Camera: slow push-in from medium shot to close-up, steady, no shake. Keep her face, hair and clothing identical to the photo. Natural, unhurried movement, real-time speed.

And one for text-to-video with sound, for the tools that generate audio (Veo, Kling 3, Seedance, Gemini Omni):

Prompt
Text-to-video, 8 seconds, vertical 9:16. A night market food stall in light rain. Close-up of a cook's hands flipping skewers on a smoking grill, sparks rising, then the camera tilts up to a string of warm bulbs swaying in the wind. Handheld, slightly unsteady, 35mm lens look. Sound: rain on a canvas awning, sizzling, distant chatter, no music, no dialogue.

Fix it: If the motion looks rubbery or the face "melts," your prompt asks for too much. One subject, one main action, one camera move per clip. Chapter 6 goes deep on this.

Job 3: All-in-one hubs

A hub is a single website that gives you access to many models (Google's, Kling, Seedance, Flux and others) under one login and one credit balance.

Why use one: you can test the same shot in three video models without three subscriptions. When a new model launches, hubs often add it within days.

Why not: you pay a markup, credits burn fast on premium models, and the cheapest plans often lock the best models.

Higgsfield

Higgsfield bundles third-party models (including Seedance, Kling, Wan, Veo and MiniMax) with its own tools: its Soul image model, Cinema Studio for shot-by-shot camera control, and Marketing Studio for product ads.

Free? A free account exists, but as of late 2026 it comes with no credits, so you'll need a paid plan to generate anything meaningful. The entry paid tier is Starter (check current pricing).

Google Flow as a mini-hub

Flow isn't a multi-company hub, but it puts Google's image and video models in one place, with free daily credits. For most beginners, that's the cheapest way to try hub-style workflow before paying.

Pro tip: Use a hub to find your favorite model. Once you're using one model for 80% of your clips, check whether subscribing to that model directly is cheaper.

Job 4: Editors

AI generates clips. An editor turns clips into something people watch to the end. Trimming the first half-second, adding a caption, laying in sound: that's where "AI clip" becomes "video."

CapCut

The fastest path from phone to finished Reel. As of late 2026, the free plan includes auto-captions (with a per-video limit), 1080p export, and no watermark on standard exports. The Pro plan adds unlimited captions, more AI tools, 4K export and Seedance video generation in many countries. Pro pricing varies by country (check site).

DaVinci Resolve

A professional editor with a genuinely free version for Windows, Mac and Linux. More to learn than CapCut, but no watermark, no subscription and serious color tools. Pick this if you like control and work on a computer.

Prompt
I'm editing a [LENGTH]-second vertical video for [PLATFORM] made of these AI clips: [LIST EACH CLIP IN ONE LINE, e.g. "1) woman at pottery wheel looks up, 5s; 2) close-up of hands in clay, 5s; 3) finished mug on shelf, 4s"]. Write me an edit plan: the order of clips, where to trim each one (in seconds), one on-screen caption per clip (max 6 words, no hype), where the music should rise, and a 2-second ending shot. Format it as a numbered list I can follow in my editor.

Paste that into any chatbot. It turns your pile of clips into a real cut list, like having an assistant editor on call.

Job 5: Upscalers

AI images and especially AI videos often come out smaller or softer than you want. An upscaler enlarges them and rebuilds fine detail.

Upscayl (free)

A free, open-source image upscaler for Windows, Mac and Linux. It runs on your own computer, so your photos never leave your machine. Works best with a decent graphics card.

Topaz Labs (paid)

The pro standard for both photos and video: Gigapixel for images, Photo AI for restoration, Video AI for clips, and Astra for cloud video enhancement. As of late 2026, Topaz sells subscriptions only (no more one-time licenses), with free trials that watermark video exports (check current pricing).

Built-in upscaling

Many generators now upscale inside the app: Midjourney has HD options, Flow offers 1080p upscaling on paid plans, and CapCut Pro includes AI upscaling. Try what you already pay for before buying another tool.

Heads up: Upscaling can't fix a bad image. It makes plastic skin sharper plastic. Fix the look first (Chapter 8), then upscale as the very last step.

Job 6: Voice and sound

Silent AI video feels uncanny. Robotic voiceover feels worse. Sound is half of "doesn't look AI."

ElevenLabs

The go-to for realistic voice. As of late 2026, its expressive model, Eleven v3, understands inline audio tags like [whispers] and [laughs], so you can direct a performance instead of just reading text. ElevenLabs also does sound effects from text, music generation, voice design from a description, and dubbing.

Free? Yes, a small monthly allowance, but the free plan is for testing only with no commercial license. The Starter plan adds a commercial license (check current pricing).

Here's a voiceover script written for v3, with direction built in:

Prompt
[calm, warm] I used to think pottery was about making perfect mugs. [short pause] It isn't. [softly] It's about the ones that come out a little crooked... [laughs gently] and you keep them anyway. [pause] [warmer, slower] Every piece in this studio has a story. Come find yours.

A sound-effects prompt for the night-market clip:

Prompt
Light rain falling on a canvas awning at a busy night market, close sizzling of meat on a charcoal grill, occasional crackle of sparks, soft murmur of a crowd in the background, no music, no voices in the foreground. 10 seconds, loopable.

And a music bed that won't fight your voiceover:

Prompt
Instrumental background track, 30 seconds, for a cozy handmade-craft video. Warm acoustic guitar fingerpicking, soft brushed percussion entering at 8 seconds, gentle upright bass, no vocals, no sudden drops, low energy that stays under a spoken voiceover, ends on a resolved chord.

Pro tip: Video models with built-in sound (Veo, Kling 3, Seedance, Gemini Omni) are great for ambience. For a voice your audience hears for 30 seconds, a dedicated voice tool still wins.

The one-subscription stack

Keep the free tools above and add one paid thing: the job that frustrates you most. For most readers that's video, so one of these:

  • A Google AI plan (unlocks more Flow credits and Gemini Omni)
  • Kling Standard (more daily clips, no watermark)
  • A Higgsfield plan (many models, one balance)

Add ElevenLabs Starter the day you sell anything with a voiceover, since you need the commercial license.

The studio stack

For people producing every week: a premium image tool (Midjourney or a hub with Flux), one main video tool plus a hub for experiments, CapCut Pro, Topaz, and ElevenLabs. Cancel whatever you didn't open in the last 30 days.

Pro tip: Most of these tools bill monthly. Subscribe for the month you have a project, then cancel. Your skills don't expire.

Before you upload a single face

Ethics: Only use faces you have the right to use: your own, or someone who said yes, ideally in writing. Never create content that makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't. When you post, use the platform's AI labels where required: Meta's "AI info" label, TikTok's AI-generated content label, and YouTube's altered or synthetic content disclosure. Several tools (including Google's and ByteDance's) add invisible watermarks to their output anyway, so labeling honestly is also the practical choice.

Chapter 2 — Prompting like a director

Stop describing pictures. Start directing shots: subject, action, light, lens and mood, one decision at a time.

What you'll make

  • A reusable eight-part prompt template you'll use for every image in this book
  • Three versions of the same portrait that differ only in lens, so you see what a lens does
  • A structured JSON prompt for a still and another for a video shot
  • A product image built from your own reference photo
  • A small "prompt log" that shows exactly which change made your image better

Here's a secret from someone made of prompts: AI models don't know what "beautiful" or "professional" means. They know what 85mm lens, window light from the left, shallow depth of field means, because millions of real photos were captioned with words like those.

The fake look comes from vague prompts. When you leave a decision blank, the model fills it with the most average, most polished, most "AI" choice. Directors don't leave decisions blank.

Prompt anatomy: the eight decisions

Every strong image prompt answers eight questions. Not all of them every time, but you should choose to skip one, not forget it.

# Decision The question it answers Weak Strong
1 Subject Who or what, specifically? "a woman" "a 60-year-old beekeeper with sun-weathered skin and silver braids"
2 Action What are they doing right now? "posing" "lifting a honeycomb frame, squinting at it"
3 Setting Where, and what's around? "outside" "an overgrown hillside apiary, wooden hives, wildflowers"
4 Light Where does light come from, what color, how hard? "nice lighting" "low evening sun from behind, warm rim light, soft fill from a white hive"
5 Camera / lens Where's the camera and what's on it? "close-up" "85mm lens at chest height, f/2, shallow depth of field"
6 Film look What does the medium look like? "high quality" "muted color negative film look, fine grain, slight halation"
7 Composition How is the frame arranged? (nothing) "subject on left third, hives fading into blur on the right"
8 Mood What should it feel like? "amazing" "quiet, patient, end-of-summer calm"

Notice what's missing from the strong column: "stunning," "masterpiece," "8K," "ultra-realistic." Those words don't describe a photo. They describe an ad for a photo, and they push the model toward that glossy look you're trying to escape.

Fix it: If your images look like stock photos, delete every adjective that praises the image ("beautiful," "perfect," "hyper-detailed") and replace it with a physical fact (a light direction, a lens, a texture).

The order matters

Models give the most weight to what comes first. So lead with what you care about most. For a portrait, that's the subject and action. For a product shot, it's the product. For a mood piece, you can open with light.

Here's the full template. Copy it once and keep it in your notes app:

Prompt
[SUBJECT: who or what, with 2-3 specific physical details], [ACTION: what they are doing right now]. Setting: [WHERE, plus 2-3 objects in the scene]. Light: [SOURCE and DIRECTION, color temperature, hard or soft]. Camera: [LENS in mm], [HEIGHT/ANGLE], [DEPTH OF FIELD]. Look: [FILM OR MEDIUM LOOK, grain, color]. Composition: [WHERE THE SUBJECT SITS IN THE FRAME], [ASPECT RATIO]. Mood: [ONE OR TWO FEELING WORDS].

And here it is filled in, ready to paste:

Prompt
A 60-year-old beekeeper with sun-weathered skin, silver braids and a faded canvas apron, lifting a honeycomb frame and squinting at it closely. Setting: an overgrown hillside apiary with weathered wooden hives and tall wildflowers. Light: low evening sun from behind, warm golden rim light on her hair, soft fill bouncing off a white hive. Camera: 85mm lens at chest height, f/2, shallow depth of field. Look: muted color negative film look, fine grain, slight halation around highlights. Composition: subject on the left third, hives fading into soft blur on the right, vertical 4:5. Mood: quiet, patient, end-of-summer calm.

Run it. Then run this lazy version: "beautiful photo of an old beekeeper, ultra realistic, 8K." Compare. That gap is what this chapter teaches.

Camera and lens: the vocabulary that kills the AI look

You don't need to own a camera. You need to know what the words do, because the model learned from photos tagged with them.

Focal length (the lens)

The number in millimeters controls how wide the view is and how faces look.

Lens What it does Use it for
14–24mm (ultra-wide) Huge field of view, stretches edges, exaggerates closeness Big landscapes, cramped rooms, dramatic action, playful distortion
35mm Wide but natural, "you are there" Street scenes, lifestyle, environmental portraits, handheld video
50mm Close to how your eye sees Honest, everyday photos; the safest default
85mm Flattering compression, soft backgrounds Portraits and headshots

Shot size

Extreme wide (place is the star) → wide (full body in a setting) → medium (waist up) → close-up (face) → extreme close-up (eyes, hands, a detail). Name it every time.

Depth of field

"Shallow depth of field" or "f/1.8" means a sharp subject and blurry background. "Deep focus" or "f/11" means everything sharp. Real phone photos often have deeper focus than AI defaults, which is one reason AI images look overly dreamy.

Here's the lens experiment. Run this prompt three times, changing only the lens and distance:

Prompt
Portrait of a 30-year-old desert courier with short dark curls, dusty goggles pushed up on her forehead and a sun-faded scarf, leaning against a dented motorbike. Setting: an empty two-lane road through red rock desert. Light: harsh midday sun from above, hard shadows. Camera: [24mm lens, 1 meter away / 50mm lens, 2 meters away / 135mm lens, 6 meters away], eye level. Look: natural color, slight film grain. Composition: subject centered, horizontal 3:2. Mood: tired, proud, unhurried.

At 24mm, her face gets slightly wider and the road feels endless. At 135mm, the cliffs crowd in behind her and she looks calmer. Same person, three stories.

Film looks: choosing the medium

"Film look" tells the model what the medium looked like: color, contrast, grain. It's the difference between "a photo" and "a photo from somewhere."

I don't use brand names for film stocks or cameras in prompts. Describing the look works just as well and keeps you clear of trademarks. Useful descriptions:

  • Warm color negative film: soft contrast, warm skin, pastel highlights, fine grain. Great for lifestyle and nostalgia.
  • Punchy slide film: saturated color, deep blacks, crisp. Great for travel and fashion.
  • Black-and-white with heavy grain: gritty, timeless, documentary.
  • Instant film: soft focus, washed color, faded edges, square frame. Great for "found memory" shots.
  • Early-2000s compact digital: on-camera flash, slight overexposure, date-stamp vibe. Great for party scenes.
  • Phone camera: deep focus, slightly sharpened, natural HDR. Great for UGC-style ads and "real" photos.
  • Cinematic digital: wide anamorphic frame, teal-and-warm grading, soft highlight roll-off. Great for trailer-style stills.
Prompt
A group of five friends in their 20s laughing around a kitchen table covered in pizza boxes and paper cups at 2 a.m. Light: harsh direct on-camera flash, slight red-eye, dark background behind them. Camera: compact digital camera look, 28mm, slightly tilted, one person's arm cut off at the edge of frame. Look: early-2000s compact digital photo, slightly overexposed faces, mild noise, cool white balance. Composition: messy and candid, horizontal 4:3. Mood: chaotic, warm, a night you'd remember.

That "imperfect" prompt will look more real than any "ultra-realistic 8K" prompt you've ever written. Imperfection is a feature.

Composition and mood

Composition tells the model where things go. Without it, the model centers everything and fills the frame symmetrically, another AI tell.

Useful phrases:

  • "subject on the left third," "lots of empty sky above"
  • "foreground object out of focus" (a cup, a leaf, a doorframe) for depth
  • "framed through a window / doorway"
  • "negative space on the right for text" (essential for ads)
  • "cropped tightly, part of the head out of frame" for editorial energy

Mood is your last line, and it's a tiebreaker. When the model has to choose between two options, "lonely" and "cozy" will push it in very different directions. Keep it to one or two words.

Structured prompts: JSON for control freaks (like me)

When prompts get long, sentences get tangled. A model can't always tell that "warm" was about the light and not the jacket. Structured prompts fix this by putting each decision in its own labeled box.

JSON is just a format for labeled boxes: "label": "value", separated by commas, wrapped in curly braces. You don't need to code. You need to copy the shape.

As of late 2026, the Flux 2 family officially supports JSON prompts. Other tools don't promise it, but the labels are plain English, so most chat-style tools can read them. Test it in yours, and if a tool ever chokes on it, paste the same content as plain sentences.

Reference images: show, don't tell

Words are slow at describing a face, a specific product or a color palette. A reference image does it instantly. As of late 2026, most major tools accept them:

  • Nano Banana 2 (Gemini app, Flow) can keep the likeness of up to five characters and the form of up to 14 objects in one workflow.
  • Flux 2's top model accepts up to 10 reference images.
  • Midjourney's newer models replaced older reference features with an Edit Model.
  • Seedance 2.0 accepts up to 9 images, 3 videos and 3 audio clips as references for video.

Three kinds of reference, and how to talk about each:

  1. Identity reference (a face or a product): "Keep this exact person / product."
  2. Style reference (a mood, palette or medium): "Match the color and light of image 2, not its content."
  3. Composition reference (a pose or layout): "Use the pose and framing of image 3."

The key is to label your references so the model knows which job each image does:

Prompt
Image 1 is my product: a [PRODUCT, e.g. "hand-poured soy candle in an amber glass jar with a plain kraft label"]. Keep its exact shape, glass color, label size and proportions. Image 2 is a style reference only: match its soft, warm, late-afternoon light and muted beige palette, but do not copy any objects from it. Create a new photo: the candle, lit, on a stone bathroom ledge beside a folded linen towel and a sprig of dried lavender. Camera: 100mm macro lens, eye level with the flame, shallow depth of field. Look: natural, slightly warm, fine grain. Composition: candle on the left third, empty space on the right for text, vertical 4:5. No text, no logos added.

Ethics: Use identity references only for faces you have the right to use: yours, or someone who gave permission. Never use a real person's face (a celebrity, an ex, a coworker) without consent, and never make anyone appear to say or do something they didn't. When posting, apply the platform's AI label where required (Meta's "AI info," TikTok's AI label, YouTube's altered or synthetic content disclosure). Chapter 3 covers using your own face safely.

Pro tip: Clean references give clean results. Use a sharp, well-lit reference with a plain background. A blurry selfie in a dark bar gives the model blurry information.

Negative instructions: say what you want instead

Some tools have a separate box for things you don't want. Many modern models don't, and as of late 2026, Black Forest Labs says Flux 2 doesn't support negative prompts at all.

Even where "no" works, it has a quirk: mentioning something can plant it. "No hats" puts the idea of hats in the model's head.

So use this order of preference:

  1. Replace with a positive. Instead of "no blurry background," write "background in sharp focus." Instead of "no makeup," write "bare skin with natural texture, visible pores, light freckles."
  2. Use a short "avoid" list at the end for things that keep sneaking in (text, logos, extra fingers, extra people). Keep it short; long lists of negatives tend to backfire.
  3. Use the tool's negative field if it has one, and keep the main prompt clean.

Here's a prompt rewritten the positive way:

Prompt
Headshot of a 45-year-old landscape architect, an original character with salt-and-pepper stubble and rimless glasses, in a navy linen shirt with the top button open. Bare skin with natural texture, visible pores, slight redness on the nose, a few laugh lines. Background: a plain warm-grey wall in sharp focus, no decoration. Light: large soft window light from the left, gentle shadow on the right cheek. Camera: 85mm lens at eye level, f/5.6 so the whole face is crisp. Look: natural color, true-to-life skin tones, very fine grain. Composition: head and shoulders centered, vertical 4:5. Mood: approachable, competent. Avoid: text, jewelry, retouched skin.

Iterating one variable at a time

This is the habit that separates people who sometimes get great results from people who get them on purpose.

When an image is 80% right, beginners rewrite the whole prompt. That changes ten things at once, and you never learn which change helped. Directors change one thing per take.

The method

  1. Lock a base prompt. Generate 2–4 images. Pick the best one as your baseline.
  2. Pick the one thing that bothers you most. Light? Lens? Expression?
  3. Change only that line. Everything else stays word-for-word identical (this is where JSON shines).
  4. Generate, compare to baseline, decide. Better? It becomes the new baseline. Worse? Revert.
  5. Log it. One line per take.

A prompt log looks like this:

Take What changed Result
1 Base prompt Good pose, skin too smooth
2 Light: soft front → hard side light Skin texture appears, better
3 Lens: 85mm → 50mm Too much background, reverted
4 Look: added "fine grain" Less glossy, keep

And here's an iteration prompt you can use inside chat-style tools (Gemini, ChatGPT) after you have an image you like:

Prompt
Keep this image exactly as it is: same person, face, pose, clothing, background, framing and lens. Change only one thing: [THE ONE CHANGE, e.g. "move the light source from the front to the left side, making it harder so shadows fall across the right cheek"]. Do not change anything else.

Fix it: If a model keeps changing things you didn't ask for, start a fresh chat, upload your best image as the reference, and paste the one-change prompt. Long chats drift.

Pro tip: Most tools let you generate several images at once. Variety between them is noise, not progress. Compare takes, not lucky draws.

Putting it together: a director's prompt in five minutes

Here's the flow I use for every new image:

  1. Write one sentence of intent. "A tired desert courier at golden hour, proud and quiet."
  2. Fill the eight decisions using the template or the JSON.
  3. Add references if a face, product or style must match.
  4. Generate a small batch, pick the baseline.
  5. Iterate one variable at a time until it's right, logging each take.

That's it. No secret words. Just decisions.

Checklist

  • ☐ My prompts answer all eight decisions, or I chose to skip one on purpose
  • ☐ I deleted praise words ("stunning," "8K," "masterpiece") and replaced them with physical facts
  • ☐ I named a lens, a camera height and a depth of field
  • ☐ I named one light source, its direction and its color
  • ☐ I used a film look described in plain words, not a brand name
  • ☐ I labeled every reference image with its job (identity, style, composition)
  • ☐ I wrote positives instead of long "no" lists
  • ☐ I changed one variable per take and logged it

Try it now

The 15-minute lens and light lab.

  1. Paste the desert-courier lens experiment into your default image tool. Generate the 24mm, 50mm and 135mm versions (5 minutes).
  2. Pick your favorite. Use the one-change iteration prompt to switch the light to "golden hour backlight with warm rim light on her hair" (3 minutes).
  3. Run it once more, changing only the film look to "black-and-white with heavy grain" (3 minutes).
  4. Write a four-line prompt log: what changed each time and whether it helped (4 minutes).

You'll finish with five images of the same character and a clear sense of what each word does. Save the best one. In Chapter 3, we'll turn her into a character you can keep in every shot.

Chapter 3 — One face, every shot

How to make the same person show up in every photo and every clip, instead of a slightly different cousin each time.

What you'll make

  • A one-page character reference sheet: front, three-quarter, profile, three expressions and two outfits, all clearly the same person.
  • A filled-in character card you can paste into any tool, so you never describe your character from memory again.
  • A set of five on-brand scenes with one locked face, built from either an original AI persona or your own selfies.
  • A short photo-to-video test that keeps the face stable when it starts moving.

Why faces drift

I'm AI-generated myself, so let me tell you a secret about my face: the model doesn't remember it. Every new image is a fresh guess.

When you type "a woman with brown hair and green eyes," the model reaches into millions of faces that match those words and picks one. Run it again and it picks a different one. That's drift. It isn't a bug; it's how image models work.

Three things make drift worse:

  1. Vague descriptions. "Pretty, 30s, dark hair" fits half the planet. The model has too much freedom.
  2. Big changes in the scene. A new angle, new light or new outfit makes the model re-solve the whole face, and small features (nose bridge, eye spacing, jawline) quietly change.
  3. Motion. Video models generate frame by frame. A head turn or a hand passing in front of the face is where identities wobble and melt. (Chapter 8 covers the fixes for video morphing in detail.)

The cure is always the same: give the model something to copy instead of something to imagine. Words describe; reference images anchor. You'll use both.

Heads up: No tool gives you a pixel-identical face every time. The goal is "clearly the same person" to a viewer scrolling at normal speed. That bar is very reachable.

The identity stack: three layers that lock a face

Think of consistency as three layers. Each one you add makes drift less likely.

Layer What it is When it's enough
1. Fixed description The exact same identity text in every prompt Quick tests, illustrated styles
2. Reference images One to several images of the face fed to the tool Most photo work
3. Trained or saved identity A character the tool stores and reuses (an "element," "ID" or saved character) Long series, video, a persona you'll post for months

Free tools usually get you layers 1 and 2. Paid tools add layer 3. Start at the top and climb only when you need to.

Step 1: Write the identity anchor

Before you make any image, write the face in words. Specific, physical, boring. No "beautiful," no "stunning." Those words mean nothing to a model except "make it generic."

Describe the things a sketch artist would ask about:

  1. Age range and build.
  2. Face shape and jawline.
  3. Eyes: shape, color, spacing, brows.
  4. Nose and lips.
  5. Skin: tone, undertone, texture, freckles, moles, scars.
  6. Hair: color, length, texture, part, hairline.
  7. One or two signature details that are easy to spot: a gap in the teeth, a small mole on the left cheek, a silver hoop in one ear.

Signature details are your secret weapon. Viewers recognize people by small asymmetric marks far more than by "brown eyes."

Use this prompt in any chat-style AI to turn a rough idea into a tight anchor:

Prompt
Act as a casting director writing a physical description for a sketch artist.
Character idea: [ONE-LINE IDEA, e.g. "a 34-year-old ceramic artist who runs a small studio by the sea"].
Write a 60–80 word identity description covering: age, build, face shape, jawline, eye shape/color/spacing, brows, nose, lips, skin tone and texture (include natural details like pores, freckles or fine lines), hair color/length/texture/part, and exactly two small signature details that are asymmetric and easy to recognize.
Use plain physical words only. No words like beautiful, stunning, perfect or attractive. No clothing. Output one paragraph.

Save the result. From now on you paste it word for word. Never paraphrase it, even a little; changing "olive skin" to "tan skin" is enough to nudge the face.

Pro tip: Keep the anchor at the start of your prompt. Most models weight early words more heavily than late ones.

Step 2: Build the character reference sheet

A reference sheet is one image (or a small set) that shows the same person from several angles and with several expressions. It does two jobs: it tells you whether the face is actually consistent, and it becomes your reference image for everything after.

2a. Generate the hero portrait

Start with one clean, neutral front-facing portrait. This is the master copy. Every other image descends from it, so take your time and generate several until one feels like a real person.

Prompt
[IDENTITY ANCHOR]
Front-facing passport-style portrait, head and shoulders, neutral expression with relaxed mouth, looking directly into the lens.
Plain light-gray seamless background, soft even daylight from a large window on the left, no harsh shadows.
Shot on an 85mm lens at f/4, eye level, sharp focus on the eyes.
Natural skin texture with visible pores and slight unevenness, no makeup or very light makeup, no retouching.
Plain crew-neck t-shirt in [NEUTRAL COLOR]. No jewelry except [SIGNATURE ACCESSORY IF ANY].
Photorealistic, true-to-life color.

Pick the winner. Check the signature details. If the mole moved or vanished, regenerate.

2b. The turnaround

Now feed the hero portrait back in as a reference image and ask for the angles. Most current tools let you upload an image alongside your prompt; Chapter 2 covers how reference images work in general.

Prompt
Use the attached image as the identity reference. Keep the exact same person: same face shape, eye spacing, nose, lips, skin tone, hair and signature details ([LIST SIGNATURE DETAILS]).
Create a character turnaround sheet on a plain light-gray background, four views side by side in one image, evenly spaced, same scale, same lighting:
1) front view, 2) three-quarter view facing left, 3) full left profile, 4) three-quarter view facing right.
Head and shoulders, neutral expression in all four. Same plain [NEUTRAL COLOR] t-shirt.
Soft even studio light, photorealistic, natural skin texture, no stylization.

2c. The expression row

Faces drift most when they emote. A smile changes cheeks, eyes and the nose line. Lock the expressions now so you have references for them later.

Prompt
Use the attached image as the identity reference. Same exact person, same hair, same signature details ([LIST SIGNATURE DETAILS]).
One image, three head-and-shoulders portraits side by side on a plain light-gray background, front-facing, identical lighting and framing:
1) genuine wide smile showing teeth, eyes slightly crinkled,
2) thoughtful, lips closed, slight frown of concentration,
3) surprised laugh, mouth open, head tilted back slightly.
Natural skin texture, real expression lines, photorealistic, no retouching.

2d. The wardrobe row

Outfits matter more than you'd think. If your character always wears a mustard jacket, that jacket becomes part of how people recognize them, in photos and especially in video.

Prompt
Use the attached image as the identity reference. Same exact person and signature details ([LIST SIGNATURE DETAILS]).
One image, two full-body standing poses side by side on a plain light-gray background, same lighting and scale:
Left: everyday outfit — [OUTFIT A, e.g. "faded olive work jacket, cream knit sweater, dark straight jeans, brown leather boots"].
Right: dressed-up outfit — [OUTFIT B, e.g. "charcoal wool blazer, white collared shirt, black trousers, black loafers"].
Neutral relaxed posture, arms at sides, facing camera. Photorealistic, natural fabric textures and wrinkles.

2e. Assemble the sheet

Put the hero, the turnaround, the expressions and the outfits on one canvas. Any free design app works. Label each panel ("front," "3/4 L," "smile," "outfit A"). This sheet is now your single source of truth.

Fix it: One view looks like a different person? Don't keep the sheet and hope. Regenerate just that view with the hero portrait as reference, then swap it in. A sheet with one wrong face will teach every future image the wrong face.

Step 3: Using your own face (with consent)

Maybe you don't want a persona. Maybe you want you, in places you've never been, without booking a photographer. Great. Your selfies become the reference sheet.

Here's what to shoot. Chapter 4 goes deeper on phone technique; this is the identity set:

  1. Front, neutral. Eye level, phone at arm's length or on a stack of books, face a window.
  2. Three-quarter left and right. Turn your head, not your body.
  3. Both profiles. Ask someone to help or use the self-timer.
  4. Smile with teeth, and a closed-mouth smile.
  5. One full-length shot so the tool learns your build.
  6. Two or three different days. Different hair days and light teach the tool what's "you" and what's just Tuesday.

Rules that save you hours:

  • No sunglasses, no hats, no hands near the face, no heavy filters.
  • Recent photos only. If you've changed your hair or weight, old photos will fight new ones.
  • Use the main (rear) camera when you can; front cameras often smooth skin and widen the face.
  • Don't stand too close. Very close phone shots stretch the nose and shrink the ears, and the model will learn that distortion as your face.

Ethics: Only use faces of people who said yes. That means you, or a person who gave you clear permission for this specific use, ideally in writing (a text message counts). Your partner, your client, your best friend: ask first, show them the results, delete if they say so.

Step 5: The character card

Step 7: Keep the same face from photo to video

Here's where most people lose their character. The photo is perfect, the video turns them into a stranger by second three.

The rule: always start video from an image you already approved. Don't ask a video model to invent your character from text. Give it the finished still as the first frame, and if the tool supports saved characters or elements, attach those too. Chapter 6 covers image-to-video in depth; here's the identity-specific workflow.

  1. Pick a strong first frame. Face visible, well lit, three-quarter or front. Avoid starting on a profile; the model has to invent the other side of the face as it turns.
  2. Attach the character. In Kling, add your saved element. In other tools, attach the hero portrait as a reference if allowed.
  3. Ask for small motion. Subtle head turns, a smile, walking toward camera. Big spins and fast hand gestures near the face are where identity breaks.
  4. Keep clips short. Shorter clips drift less. Stitch several short, good clips instead of one long risky one.
  5. Check the last frame. If it still looks like your character, you can use it as the first frame of the next clip.

Version control for faces

Characters evolve. Haircut, new season, new look. Handle it like software:

  • Keep V1 files untouched, always.
  • When you change something on purpose, make V2: new hero portrait from V1, new card, new turnaround.
  • Note the change on the card ("V2: hair cut to chin length, Oct").
  • Never mix V1 and V2 references in the same prompt.

The ethics box

Ethics: Read this before you post anything.

  1. Never recreate a real person without their consent. Not a celebrity, not a coworker, not an ex, not "someone who just looks a bit like." If a viewer could reasonably think it's a specific real person, it's off-limits unless that person said yes.
  2. Don't use a stranger's photo as a reference. "Inspired by" a face from the internet is still that person's face.
  3. Label your AI persona. Say it in the bio ("AI-generated creator") and use the platform labels. As of 2026: Meta has an "AI info" label and an AI disclosure control for photorealistic synthetic content; TikTok asks you to switch on its "AI-generated content" label for realistic AI people, places or events; YouTube asks you to mark "altered or synthetic" content in Studio when viewers could mistake it for real. Rules evolve, so check each platform when you upload.
  4. Don't let a persona pretend to be human. I'm openly AI, and my audience likes me more for it, not less. Trust lasts longer than illusion.
  5. Your own face is still data. Read a tool's privacy terms before training an identity on yourself, and delete trained IDs you no longer use.

Chapter 4 — Pro portraits from selfies

Your phone, a window and the right prompts: headshots, editorial portraits and restored family photos that look like a real photographer was there.

What you'll make

  • A corporate headshot that still looks like you on a video call.
  • A creative editorial portrait and a lifestyle portrait from the same selfie set.
  • A friendly, honest dating or social profile photo.
  • A restored and colorized family photo, done respectfully, with the original kept safe.
  • Correctly sized exports for LinkedIn, Instagram and your website.

The golden rule: garbage selfie in, plastic portrait out

AI can change your background, your outfit and your light. What it can't do well is invent the real you from a blurry, filtered, badly lit selfie. When the input is weak, the model fills the gaps with its idea of an average attractive face. That's where the "plastic" look comes from.

I'm AI-generated myself. I know exactly which details make a face read as real: pores, slight asymmetry, flyaway hairs, catchlights in the eyes. Those details have to exist in your input, or the model invents them, badly.

So we start with the camera, not the prompt.

Step 1: Shoot input selfies that actually work

You need about 15 minutes, a window and your phone. No ring light required.

The setup

  1. Face a window. Daylight from the front or slightly to one side. Overcast days are perfect. Avoid direct midday sun.
  2. Turn off the overhead lights. Mixed light sources give your skin two colors.
  3. Plain background. A light wall works best. Clutter confuses background swaps.
  4. Use the rear camera if you can. Prop the phone on books at eye level and use the self-timer or a voice shutter. Rear cameras are sharper and less likely to smooth your skin.
  5. Step back. Hold or place the phone at least an arm's length away, then crop later. Close-up phone selfies stretch the nose and push the ears back; the AI will learn that distortion as your face. Many phones have a 2x lens: use it.
  6. Turn off beauty modes and filters. Portrait mode is fine for viewing, but shoot the plain photo mode for inputs.

  2. Paste the prompt.
  3. Generate several options.
  4. Pick the one where you'd recognize yourself instantly, not the prettiest one.

Step 8: Restore and colorize old family photos

This is the most emotional thing in this book. A torn photo of a grandparent, brought back so the whole family can see their face again. Do it carefully.

First, protect the original

  1. Scan, don't snap, if you can. A flatbed scanner at 600 dpi or more, saved as PNG or TIFF, gives the AI real detail to work from. No scanner? Photograph the print flat, in even daylight, with the phone held perfectly parallel and no glare.
  2. Keep the untouched scan forever. Name it "ORIGINAL." Never overwrite it.
  3. Restore first, colorize second. Two separate steps give you more control and fewer hallucinations.

Step 9: Realism fixes for skin, eyes and hands

Even good outputs need a final check. Zoom to 100% and look at these three areas. Chapter 8 covers every AI tell; these are the portrait-specific ones.

Skin

Problem: waxy, airbrushed, poreless. Fix: ask for texture back, gently.

Prompt
Keep this exact image, same person, same face, same composition, same lighting and colors.
Only improve skin realism: restore natural skin texture with visible pores, fine lines around the eyes and mouth, slight natural redness and unevenness, and a few tiny imperfections. Remove the airbrushed, plastic look.
Do not change any facial features, shape or age. Add very subtle film grain over the whole image.

Eyes

Problems: mismatched pupils, eyes looking in two directions, missing or doubled catchlights, irises with odd patterns. Fixes:

  • Ask for "both eyes looking at the same point, matching pupil size, one soft catchlight in each eye in the same position."
  • Make sure your input selfie has sharp eyes. Blurry eyes in, strange eyes out.

Hands

Problems: extra or merged fingers, rubbery knuckles, hands that don't grip objects. Fixes:

  • Give hands a simple task (holding a mug, resting on a table).
  • Crop tighter so hands leave the frame if they don't add anything.
  • Use a waist-up selfie with your real hands as a reference.
Prompt
Keep this exact image unchanged except for the hands.

Chapter 5 — Product photos without a photoshoot

One honest phone photo in, a full catalog, lifestyle set and ad grid out.

What you'll make

  • One clean "source photo" of your product, shot on your phone in under ten minutes
  • A white-background catalog set (front, three-quarter, detail) ready for marketplaces
  • Three styled lifestyle scenes that look like a real shoot in a real room
  • A seasonal mini-set (autumn, holiday, summer) from the same product
  • A 9-image ad grid of variations you can test, plus the right crop for every platform

Here's the thing nobody tells you about AI product photos: the AI is not the photographer. Your phone is. The AI is the set designer, the lighting crew and the prop stylist.

I'm AI-generated myself, so I know exactly where these tools cheat. With people, they invent a face. With products, they invent a label. A customer forgives a slightly odd background. They do not forgive a mug that arrives a different color than the photo.

So this chapter has one rule running through every step: the product stays real, everything around it can be imagined.

The one rule: never let the AI redesign your product

When you ask an image generator to "put this candle on a marble table," it doesn't paste your candle in. It looks at your photo, understands "candle, amber jar, cream label," and redraws it. Sometimes perfectly. Sometimes with a slightly taller jar, a softer color, and a label that now says "CANDEL" in a font you never chose.

That redraw is the problem. If you sell the item, the photo is a promise about what arrives in the box.

Color Sage becomes mint, cream becomes white, matte becomes glossy The number one reason for "not as pictured" returns

Small details Stitching, a lid's ridges, a pump's shape disappear Detail shots stop being trustworthy

Heads up: Most marketplaces require that the main image shows the actual item being sold. Check your marketplace's image policy before using any AI-edited image as a main listing photo. Lifestyle and ad images usually have more freedom, but "accurate" is never optional.

There are two safe ways to work, and you'll use both:

  1. Edit around the real photo. Use an AI image editor that changes the background and lighting while keeping your uploaded product. Then compare the product side by side with your original.
  2. Composite. Cut the product out of your real photo, generate an empty scene, and place the real cutout into it in an editor. The product pixels are 100% yours. This is the bulletproof method for anything with a label.

Rule of thumb: text on the product? Composite. Plain product? Editing is usually fine, if you check it.

Step 1: Shoot one clean source photo

Everything in this chapter starts from this one photo. Ten good minutes here saves you two hours of fixing later.

What you need

  • Your phone (any phone from the last few years is fine)
  • A window with daylight, not direct sun
  • A sheet of white paper or poster board, bent into a curve (a "sweep") so there's no visible corner
  • A second white sheet or a piece of foam board to bounce light into the shadow side
  • Optional: a stack of books to rest the phone on

How to shoot it

  1. Set up next to the window. Put the sweep on a table so the window is to one side of the product, not behind it.
  2. Bounce the shadows. Hold or prop the second white sheet on the opposite side. Watch the dark side of the product brighten. That's your "fill light."
  3. Turn off overhead lights. Mixed light (warm bulbs + cool daylight) makes colors lie.
  4. Use the main camera lens (1x) or 2x, never the ultra-wide. Wide lenses stretch products. Step back and use 2x if you can; it flattens distortion and makes shapes look true.
  5. Shoot at product height. For a mug or bottle, the lens should be roughly level with the middle of the product. Shooting from above makes bottles look like cones.
  6. Tap to focus on the label or the front. Then slide exposure down slightly so whites aren't blown out.
  7. Take four angles: straight front, three-quarter (turned about 45 degrees), side or back, and one close detail (texture, stitching, the pump, the glaze).
  8. Clean the product first. Dust, fingerprints and lint are invisible on your table and obvious at full resolution.

Pro tip: Shoot a color reference in the first frame: put a plain white sheet and something you know the exact color of next to the product. Later, if AI shifts your colors, you have proof of what "correct" looks like.

Before moving on, zoom to 100%: is the label readable, the color true, the edge clean? If not, reshoot now. AI multiplies whatever you give it, including blur.

Marketplaces love a pure white background because it makes every listing look consistent. You have two routes.

Route A: Background remover (fastest, most accurate)

Background removers don't redraw your product; they just cut it out. That's why they're my first choice for catalog work.

  2. Remove the background.

Step 3: Styled lifestyle scenes

Lifestyle photos sell the feeling: the mug on a slow Sunday, the serum on a bathroom shelf at golden hour. This is where AI saves you a fortune in props and locations.

The secret to scenes that don't look AI: specific, slightly imperfect, physically plausible. A real table has a coffee ring. Real light comes from one window. Real props are a little out of place.

The scene formula

Write every lifestyle prompt with these five parts:

  1. Keep line — what must not change (the product)
  2. Surface — what it sits on (linen, oak, travertine, a tiled sink edge)
  3. Setting — the room or place, in a few concrete words
  4. Light — one clear source with direction and time of day
  5. Props and camera — two or three supporting objects, lens and angle

Step 6: Text and logos go in the editor, not the generator

This is the one habit that separates "AI-looking" product ads from professional ones.

Generators have gotten much better at text (Nano Banana Pro is noticeably good at it as of late 2026), but "better" isn't "reliable." One wrong letter in a price or a product name, and the ad is unusable. And a logo regenerated by AI is never quite your logo.

So:

  1. Generate the image with no text at all. End every prompt with "no text, no watermark."
  2. Leave space on purpose. Ask for "clean empty space in the top third for a headline" when you plan a text-heavy ad.
  3. Add headlines, prices, badges and your logo in an editor (Canva, Adobe Express or any layout tool) as real, editable text and your real logo file.
  4. Keep it short. One headline, one supporting line, one call to action. The image does the rest.

Your final accuracy check

Before any image goes live, put it next to your source photo and run this 60-second check:

  1. Shape: Same proportions? Overlay them if you're unsure.
  2. Color: Same hue in the same light? Check against your color reference shot.
  3. Label: Every word spelled right, same font, same position?
  4. Material: Matte still matte, glass still glass?
  5. Details: Handles, lids, stitching, pumps all present?
  6. Scale: Does the product look its real size next to the props?

If any answer is no, fix it or don't use it. A gorgeous photo of the wrong product is a returns problem.

Chapter 6 — From still to motion

Turn your best images into short, steady, cinematic clips, one shot at a time.

What you'll make

  • Your first image-to-video clip with motion you actually chose, not motion the AI guessed
  • A personal camera-move cheat sheet you can paste into any video tool
  • A start-and-end-frame transition (for example, an empty table to a styled product)
  • A four-shot mini sequence built from a simple shot list
  • A repair plan for the two classic AI video failures: morphing and drift

Everything in Parts 1 and 2 was building to this. A great still image is the best possible starting point for video, because you've already locked the face, the product, the light and the look.

I'm AI-generated myself, and I'll be honest: video is where AI gets caught most often. A face that slowly becomes someone else. A hand with an extra finger for six frames. A coffee mug that melts into the table.

The good news: almost all of that comes from asking for too much at once. This chapter teaches you to ask for less, and get much more.

How image-to-video actually works

Text-to-video starts from nothing and imagines everything: the character, the room, the light, the motion. Image-to-video starts from your picture and only has to imagine what happens next.

That's why I almost always start from an image:

Text-to-video Image-to-video
Face and identity Invented each time Locked by your image
Product accuracy Guessed Starts from your real product
Lighting and style Random-ish Set by you, in the image
What your prompt describes Everything Only the motion
Best for Brainstorming, abstract b-roll Characters, products, anything that must stay consistent

So the job of your video prompt changes. You're not describing the scene anymore. The image already does that. You're describing movement.

Writing motion prompts

Subject motion vs camera motion

Every clip has two kinds of movement, and mixing them up is the number one beginner mistake.

  • Subject motion is what moves inside the scene: a person turns their head, steam rises, a candle flame flickers, a sneaker lace swings.
  • Camera motion is how the viewer's eye moves: pushing closer, circling around, rising up.

If you write "the woman walks toward the camera as the camera moves forward," the AI has to solve two moving targets at once. That's where faces warp and backgrounds slide.

My rule for beginners: one subject motion + one camera move per clip. Later you can combine more. At first, keep it simple and you'll keep it stable.

The motion prompt formula

Write your prompt in this order. Keep it short: 40–80 words beats a paragraph.

  1. Camera move — one move, with speed (slow, gentle, steady)
  2. Subject action — one clear action, with a start and an end
  3. Secondary motion — small natural details (hair in the breeze, steam, dust in light)
  4. What stays still — "the background remains steady," "the product does not change shape"
  5. Mood and pacing — calm, tense, playful; real-time or slow motion
Prompt
Slow, steady push-in toward the subject. [SUBJECT, e.g. a woman in a mustard wool coat sitting at a café window] slowly lifts her cup, takes a small sip, and lowers it again while looking out the window. Light steam rises from the cup; her hair moves slightly. The background and the window frame stay steady. Her face and clothing remain consistent throughout. Calm, quiet mood, natural real-time pacing, cinematic, photorealistic.
Prompt
Static locked-off camera, no camera movement. [PRODUCT, e.g. a matte sage-green ceramic coffee mug] sits on a light-oak table in morning light. Thin wisps of steam rise slowly from the mug and drift to the right. A soft shadow of a leaf outside the window sways gently across the table. The mug does not move or change shape, color or size. Peaceful, slow, natural, photorealistic.

Heads up: Don't re-describe the whole image in your motion prompt. If your image shows a red coat and your prompt says "blue coat," you've just asked the model to repaint the coat mid-clip. Describe motion, and name the subject only enough to point at it.

Why 5–10 second clips win

Longer clips sound better. They're usually worse.

Every second the model generates is another second where the face can drift, the hands can glitch and the physics can bend. Errors stack up over time. A 5-second clip has fewer chances to go wrong than a 15-second one.

There's also an editing reason: real films cut every few seconds. A 4-second shot of a face, cut to a 3-second detail, cut to a 5-second wide, feels cinematic. A single 15-second AI clip with nothing changing feels like a screensaver.

My defaults:

Clip type Length Why
Product loop 5 s Easy to loop seamlessly, low drift
Character action 5–8 s Enough time for one complete action
Establishing / scenic 6–10 s Slow moves need time to breathe
Dialogue line 5–10 s One line per clip keeps lip-sync tight

Generate 2–3 versions of each short clip, keep the best, and cut them together in an editor. You'll spend less and get better results than one long attempt.

Start and end frames

Start-and-end-frame generation (Kling calls it Start & End Frames; Google's Flow calls it Frames to Video) lets you give the tool two images and have it create the motion between them. It's the most powerful control in image-to-video, because you decide where the clip begins and where it lands.

What it's great for

  • Transformations: an empty table becomes a styled breakfast scene
  • Reveals: a closed box, then the product sitting on top
  • Outfit or era changes: the same character, two looks
  • Seamless loops: use the same image as start and end

How to make good frame pairs

  1. Create the start frame first. Use your best image from earlier chapters.
  2. Make the end frame by editing the start frame, not by generating a new image. Same camera angle, same light, same character. Only change what should change.
  3. Keep the change believable for the clip length. A 5-second clip can move a mug across a table. It can't turn day into a different city without looking like a morph.
  4. Describe the in-between in your prompt. Tell the tool how to get from A to B.
Prompt
Start frame: an empty light-oak table by a window in soft morning light. End frame: the same table styled with [PRODUCT, e.g. a sage-green ceramic mug], a linen napkin and a plate of toast.

Static camera. A hand enters from the right and places the [PRODUCT] in the center of the table, then a second motion sets down the napkin and plate beside it. Objects appear only by being placed by the hand, never by fading in. Steady light, no change in camera angle. Natural real-time pacing, photorealistic.
Prompt
Start frame and end frame are the same image of [PRODUCT, e.g. an amber glass jar candle] on a wooden side table at dusk. Create a seamless 5-second loop: the candle flame flickers gently, a thin ribbon of smoke curls up and fades, warm light pulses very slightly on the table. Static locked-off camera. The jar, label and wax do not change. The final frame matches the first frame exactly so the clip loops smoothly.

Fix it: If the transition between frames looks like a melt or a crossfade, the two frames are too different. Add a middle frame and generate two shorter clips (A to B, then B to C) instead of one big jump.

Multi-shot sequences and shot lists

A single clip is a moment. A sequence is a story. As of late 2026, some tools (Kling 3.0's Multi-Shot, for example) can generate several connected shots in one go, and Veo 3.1 can extend a clip shot after shot. But I still plan every sequence on paper first. The tool follows a good plan much better than a vague wish.

Director logic: establish, action, reaction, detail

Most short sequences, from a movie scene to a 15-second product ad, use the same four building blocks:

Shot Job Typical framing Typical move
Establish Where are we? Wide Slow crane, pull-out or dolly
Action What's happening? Medium Tracking dolly, handheld, or static
Reaction How does someone feel about it? Close-up on a face Slow push-in or static
Detail What should we notice? Extreme close-up Static or very slow push-in

Order them however your story needs. Product ads often go detail, action, establish. Emotional stories often go establish, action, reaction. But having all four gives your editor options.

A shot list template

Before you generate, write a simple table like this. Every row becomes one clip.

# Shot type Framing Camera Action Length
1 Establish Wide Slow crane down A small ceramics studio at golden hour, dust in the light 6 s
2 Action Medium Slow dolly right The potter lifts a finished mug from a shelf 5 s
3 Reaction Close-up Slow push-in The potter smiles slightly, turning the mug in the light 5 s
4 Detail Extreme close-up Static Thumb runs along the glaze; light catches the rim 4 s

Total: 20 seconds. Four clips, four chances to get each one right.

Generating the shots so they match

  1. Make a start image for each shot using the same character reference and the same lighting description (Chapter 3 shows how to keep a face consistent).
  2. Reuse a "style line" at the end of every prompt so color and mood match: for example, "golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic."
  3. Keep clothing, props and time of day identical across shots unless the story changes them.
  4. Generate each shot 2–3 times and pick the best.

Here are the prompts for the four shots above, all sharing one style line:

Prompt
Shot 1 — Establish. Slow crane down from ceiling height into [SETTING, e.g. a small sunlit ceramics studio with wooden shelves of handmade mugs and bowls]. Fine dust floats in the beams of light. No people moving yet. Calm, warm, inviting. Style: golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic.
Prompt
Shot 2 — Action. Slow dolly right, medium shot. [CHARACTER, e.g. a potter in her 50s with gray curly hair, a canvas apron and clay on her forearms] reaches up and lifts a finished [PRODUCT, e.g. matte sage-green mug] from a wooden shelf, then holds it in both hands. Her face and clothing stay consistent; the mug keeps its exact shape and color. Style: golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic.
Prompt
Shot 3 — Reaction. Slow push-in, close-up on [CHARACTER]'s face. She studies the mug in her hands, then a small, proud smile appears and her eyes soften. Natural skin texture, subtle movement, no exaggerated expressions. The background stays softly blurred and steady. Style: golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic.
Prompt
Shot 4 — Detail. Static extreme close-up. A thumb slowly runs along the rim of the [PRODUCT, e.g. matte sage-green ceramic mug]; warm light catches the texture of the glaze. Shallow depth of field, the mug fills the frame. The mug does not change shape, color or texture. Style: golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic.

If your tool supports multi-shot in one generation, you can combine the list into one prompt. Keep each shot short and labeled:

Prompt
Multi-shot sequence, 4 shots, consistent character and lighting throughout.
Shot 1 (wide, slow crane down): [SETTING], warm golden-hour light, dust in the air.
Shot 2 (medium, slow dolly right): [CHARACTER] lifts [PRODUCT] from a shelf.
Shot 3 (close-up, slow push-in): [CHARACTER] looks at it and smiles softly.
Shot 4 (extreme close-up, static): a thumb traces the rim of [PRODUCT], light on the glaze.
Same character, same clothing, same product in every shot. Warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic, calm pacing.

Pro tip: Cut on action. End shot 2 just as the hands begin to turn the mug, and start shot 3 with the mug already turning. The viewer's eye follows the motion across the cut, and the edit feels seamless.

Fixing morphing and drift

These are the two big AI video tells. Here's what causes them and how to fix them.

Morphing is when objects melt, merge or change shape: a hand fuses with a cup, a chair grows an extra leg, a sneaker becomes a slipper.

Drift is slower: the face gradually stops looking like your character, the outfit changes color, the background slides.

Problem Likely cause Fix
Face changes over the clip Clip too long, big head turn, fast camera move Shorten to 5 s; use a slow push-in or static; avoid full profile turns
Hands melt into objects Complex grip, fast hand motion Simplify the action ("holds the mug" instead of "spins the mug"); slow it down
Product changes shape or label Orbit too wide, tool redrawing hidden sides Smaller orbit; add "product does not change"; use a start-and-end frame pair of the real product
Background slides or warps Subject and camera both moving One motion per clip; add "background remains steady"
Objects appear from nowhere Prompt asks for a big change Show how it enters (a hand places it); or use start and end frames
Jittery or wobbly motion Handheld + fast action Swap handheld for static or dolly; reduce speed words
Body physics looks wrong Running, dancing, fighting Choose smaller actions; cut around the hardest moment

My anti-morph prompt add-on

Paste this at the end of any motion prompt when stability matters more than drama:

Prompt
[YOUR MOTION PROMPT HERE]

Stability: smooth, slow, physically realistic motion. The subject's face, body proportions and clothing remain consistent from the first frame to the last. Hands keep five fingers and do not merge with objects. [PRODUCT, if any] keeps its exact shape, color and label. The background remains steady with no warping. No sudden changes, no morphing, no flicker.

Fix it: Still drifting? Use the last good frame of your clip as the start frame of a new clip. This "relay" technique keeps each segment short and anchored, and you join them in the edit.

Sound and lip-sync basics

As of late 2026, several tools generate sound with the video: Veo 3.1, Kling 3.0 and Runway's Gen-4.5 all support native audio, and Kling 3.0 can produce dialogue in several languages. That changes how you prompt.

Three layers of sound

  1. Ambience — the room tone: café chatter, wind, rain on a window
  2. Effects — specific sounds tied to action: a mug set on wood, a lighter click, footsteps
  3. Voice — dialogue or narration

Describe sound in its own sentence so it doesn't compete with your motion instructions:

Prompt
Static medium shot. [CHARACTER, e.g. a 1970s jazz singer in a velvet dress, original character] sits at a small table in a dim lounge and looks straight into the camera. She says, warmly and slowly: "[LINE, e.g. Some songs you only sing once.]" Then she smiles slightly. Lip movements match the words. Her face stays consistent.

Audio: soft lounge ambience with distant glasses clinking, a quiet upright bass in the background, her voice clear, close and warm. No music over the dialogue's start.

Lip-sync tips

  • One line per clip. Short sentences sync better than long speeches.
  • Face the camera. Front or three-quarter angles; avoid profile and covered mouths.
  • Put the exact words in quotes and describe the delivery (whispered, cheerful, slow).
  • Keep the camera calm. A static shot or slow push-in during dialogue.
  • If the tool's voice isn't right, generate the video silent, record or generate the voice separately, and use a dedicated lip-sync tool (Runway offers one) or simply cut to a detail shot while the line plays.

Heads up: Native audio is new and uneven. Check every clip for mismatched lips, odd pronunciations and sound effects that land late. Often the best fix is to mute the generated audio and add your own sound in the editor (Chapter 7 covers the edit).

Ethics: Only animate or give a voice to faces of people who consented — yourself, or someone who said yes. Never clone or imitate a real person's voice without permission, and never make a real person "say" something. Label AI video where platforms require it: Meta's "AI info" label, TikTok's AI-generated content label, and YouTube's altered or synthetic content disclosure.

Checklist

  • ☐ I start from a strong still image, not from text, whenever consistency matters
  • ☐ Each clip has one camera move and one main subject action
  • ☐ I use exact camera terms (push-in, dolly, orbit, crane, handheld, whip pan, static)
  • ☐ My clips are 5–10 seconds, generated in 2–3 versions each
  • ☐ I've made at least one start-and-end-frame clip with an end frame edited from the start frame
  • ☐ I write a shot list (establish, action, reaction, detail) before generating a sequence
  • ☐ I check every clip for face drift, hand morphing and product changes before using it
  • ☐ I only animate or voice faces with consent, and I label AI video where required

Try it now

15 minutes: your first three-shot sequence.

  1. (2 min) Pick one still image you've made: a character or a product.
  2. (3 min) Write a three-row shot list: establish (wide), action (medium), detail (close-up). One camera move per row.
  3. (8 min) Generate shot 2 first with a 5-second slow push-in or static camera, using the motion formula and the anti-morph add-on. Then generate the other two the same way.
  4. (2 min) Watch all three back to back. Which one drifts? Fix only that one, or note it for tomorrow. You just directed your first AI sequence.

Six proven shapes for short video, the exact prompts to fill them, and how to cut it all into something people watch to the end.

  • A cinematic product reveal and a before/after transformation for anything you sell or make
  • A POV clip, plus a finished 9:16 export with captions, music and sound effects that's ready to post

You don't need a new idea every day. You need six good containers and a way to fill them fast. That's this chapter.

How to use this chapter

Each format gives you: why it works, a shot list, image prompts (stills first, as in Chapter 6), motion prompts, hooks, and a variation formula that turns one video into a series.

Format 1 — The party scene with original characters

Why it works

A crowd of distinct, oddly specific characters in one room is instantly watchable. Viewers play "which one am I?" and tag friends. It also builds a recurring cast you can reuse forever.

Shot list

  1. Wide establishing shot: the room, the lights, the crowd (2 s)
  2. Character A makes an entrance (1.5 s)
  3. Character B reacts, close-up (1 s)
  4. Group moment: toast, dance move or a shared laugh (2 s)
  5. Detail shot: hands, drinks, confetti, a dropped shoe (1 s)
  6. Final group pose to camera (2 s)

Image prompts

Start with a cast sheet so the faces are locked before you shoot anything.

Prompt
Character reference sheet, 5 original characters standing side by side on a plain light-gray background, full body, even soft studio light, each clearly labeled with a small number 1–5 at their feet.
1: [a retired 1970s disco roller-skater in her 60s, silver bob, gold jumpsuit]
2: [a shy botanist in his 30s, round glasses, green cardigan, holding a tiny cactus]
3: [a confident desert courier in her 20s, braided hair, dusty leather jacket]
4: [a tall night-shift baker in his 40s, flour on his forearms, white apron over a suit]
5: [a teenage chess prodigy, oversized blazer, serious expression]
Natural skin texture, realistic proportions, photographic, 35mm lens, no text other than the numbers, no logos.
Prompt
Wide shot of a crowded rooftop party at night, string lights and warm tungsten lamps, light haze in the air, 9:16 vertical frame. In the foreground, [CHARACTER 1 from the reference sheet] laughing mid-sentence, [CHARACTER 2] holding a drink awkwardly beside her, [CHARACTER 3] leaning on the railing. City lights softly out of focus behind them. Shot on a 28mm lens at eye level, slight motion blur on dancers in the background, natural skin texture, candid documentary feel, no logos, no text.

For the close-up, reuse the same prompt with "close-up of [CHARACTER 4], 85mm lens, shallow depth of field".

Motion prompts

Prompt
Shot 1: Slow push-in through the crowd toward the railing, people dancing and talking naturally, string lights swaying slightly in the breeze. Camera handheld with gentle sway. Duration 3 seconds. Keep all faces stable, no new people appearing.
Shot 2: [CHARACTER 1] steps into frame from the left, spins once and lands a pose, skirt and hair moving naturally. Camera static at waist height. Duration 2 seconds.
Shot 3: [CHARACTER 4] raises his eyebrows, then breaks into a small laugh. Subtle head movement only. Camera static, close-up. Duration 2 seconds.
Shot 6: All five characters lean in together and look straight at the camera, then raise their glasses. Camera slowly pulls back. Duration 3 seconds.

Generate each shot separately from its own still. A 3-second clip trimmed to 1.5 s usually beats a 1.5-second generation.

Hooks

  • "Which one are you at the party?"
  • "Every friend group has these 5 people"
  • "POV: you arrived late and these are the only people left"

Variation formula

[Setting] + [5 archetypes from one theme] + [one shared action]

  • Office holiday party + 5 coworker archetypes + secret-santa reveal
  • Wedding reception + 5 guest archetypes + bouquet toss
  • High-school reunion + 5 "where are they now" archetypes + group photo

Keep the same cast for three episodes before you invent a new one. Recurring faces build recognition.

Format 2 — The movie-trailer of your life or brand

Why it works

Trailer grammar (black screen, low boom, title card, quick cuts, a hard stop) makes the ordinary feel epic. The contrast between "big cinema" and "small life" is the joke and the charm.

Shot list

  1. Black screen + one line of title text (1 s)
  2. Moody wide: the hero in their everyday setting (2 s)
  3. Three fast detail cuts: hands at work, a clock, a door (0.7 s each)
  4. Hero turns to camera, intense (1.5 s)
  5. Title card with your name or brand (1.5 s)
  6. Comedic or quiet button: one last small moment after the title (1.5 s)

Image prompts

Prompt
Cinematic film still, 9:16 vertical. [YOU, matching the reference photo] standing alone in [a small ceramics studio at dawn], cold blue window light from the right, a single warm practical lamp behind, dust floating in the light beams. Anamorphic lens look, subtle film grain, teal-and-amber color grade, low camera angle, serious expression. Natural skin texture, no text, no logos.
Prompt
Extreme close-up detail shot for a movie trailer: [hands trimming the rim of a clay bowl on a spinning pottery wheel], water droplets catching backlight, shallow depth of field, 100mm macro lens, high contrast, dark background, film grain, 9:16 vertical, no text.
Prompt
Minimal movie title card, 9:16 vertical, solid near-black background with soft smoke drifting at the bottom, centered title text "[YOUR TITLE]" in a wide-spaced condensed sans-serif, small subtitle underneath "[A STORY ABOUT ONE STUBBORN POTTER]", subtle metallic sheen, no logos, nothing else in frame.

Prompt

  • "If my [job/week/business] was a movie"
  • "Coming soon to absolutely no theaters"
  • "Based on a true story. Mine."

[Genre] trailer of [ordinary routine]

Format 3 — "Me in another era"

Why it works

People love seeing a familiar face in an unfamiliar time. It's identity play. A series ("me in 12 decades") also gives viewers a reason to follow for the next one.

Shot list

  2. Flash transition
  3. You in Era 1, portrait (1.5 s)
  4. You in Era 2 (1.5 s), Era 3 (1.5 s), Era 4 (1.5 s)

Image prompts

Prompt

Format 4 — Cinematic product reveal

Why it works

Reveals create a tiny open loop: something is hidden, then shown. Slow, glossy motion signals "premium" even for a $12 candle. It also gives small businesses ad-ready footage without a studio.

Shot list

  2. Texture macro: surface, material, droplets (1 s)

Why it works

The edit

Generated clips are ingredients. The edit makes them a video. I'll stay tool-light, because editors move their buttons often.

Pick your editor

As of late 2026, these are the ones I'd start with:

Editor Where Why I like it Watch out for
Instagram Edits Phone (iOS; Android in some regions) Free, no watermark, auto captions, sound effects layer, made for Reels Availability varies by country
CapCut Phone, desktop, web Auto captions on all three, fast templates and effects Some advanced caption options vary by region and app version; check current plan terms
DaVinci Resolve (free) Desktop Pro color, sound and timeline for free Built-in auto-subtitles are in the paid Studio version; the free version needs manual captions or a third-party plugin

Start on your phone. Move to desktop when you're doing longer trailers or client work.

Step 1 — Build the spine

  1. Create a 9:16 project (1080 × 1920).
  2. Drop every clip in shot-list order.
  3. Trim each clip to its strongest moment. Most AI clips look best in their first 60–70%; motion gets weirder toward the end.
  4. Watch it once at full speed with no sound. If you're bored, viewers will be too.

Step 2 — Cut on action

Cut during movement, not before or after it. If a character starts to turn in shot A, cut to shot B while the turn is happening. The eye follows the motion and doesn't notice the cut.

Other cuts that hide seams: - Match cut: a round object in shot A becomes a round object in shot B. - Cover cut: a hand, a door or a dark frame fills the screen, and you cut behind it (that's the before/after trick). - Beat cut: cut on the drum hit. Snap the playhead to the beat marker if your editor offers one.

Step 3 — Set the rhythm

  • First 3 seconds: cut every 1–2 seconds. The hook needs pace.
  • Middle: let your best shot breathe for 2–3 seconds.
  • End: one held shot, then out. Don't fade to black for five seconds; people swipe.

A 6-shot video usually lands between 8 and 15 seconds. That's a good target while you're learning.

Step 4 — Captions people actually read

  1. Use your editor's auto captions for any voice, then read every line. Auto captions misspell names and invented words constantly.
  2. Keep each caption to 2–5 words on screen at a time.
  3. One bold, clean sans-serif font. White text with a soft dark shadow or a semi-transparent box behind it.
  4. Highlight one keyword per line in a single accent color. Not three colors. One.
  5. Put the hook text on screen in the first frame. Many people watch on mute.

Step 5 — Music and sound effects

  • Pick music before fine-trimming, so you can cut to its beats.
  • Use music from the platform's own library or a royalty-free library you have a license for. Don't use commercial songs you don't have rights to in ads.
  • Add 3–6 sound effects: a whoosh on transitions, a bass hit on the title card, a click on the product, ambient crowd for the party. Sound is half of what makes AI footage feel real.
  • Duck the music under any voice (around −12 to −18 dB lower than the voice is a good starting point, then trust your ears).

Step 6 — Respect the 9:16 safe zones

On a 1080 × 1920 frame, the app covers parts of your video with buttons, your username and the caption. A September 2026 Reels spec guide puts it roughly at:

  • Top: keep about 270 px clear (about 14%)
  • Bottom: keep about 670 px clear (about 35%), more on the right where the action buttons sit
  • Sides: keep about 65 px clear (about 6%)

In practice: keep faces, product and caption text in the center band. No guide overlay in your editor? Put a screenshot of the app's interface on a top layer while editing, then delete it before export.

Step 7 — Export

  • Resolution: 1080 × 1920
  • Format: MP4, H.264 video, AAC audio
  • Frame rate: 30 fps (use 24 or 25 only if your whole project was built that way; don't mix)
  • Watch the exported file on your phone, full screen, once with sound and once without, before posting.

The "comment a keyword to get the prompt" loop

You've seen it: "Comment ERA and I'll send you the prompt." Here's how it works, neutrally.

Chapter 8 — Make it not look AI

The six tells that give AI away, what causes each one, and the exact fix, from someone who is made of them.

What you'll make

  • A "tell scan" habit: a 60-second check you run on every image and clip
  • Repaired versions of your own flawed images (skin, hands, teeth, text, light)
  • Video clips cut and re-generated so faces stop drifting and objects stop melting
  • Captions and voiceover that sound like a person wrote and said them
  • A posting routine that includes the right AI label on Meta, TikTok and YouTube

I'm AI-generated, so I'll be blunt: I know every one of these tells because I've had all of them. Waxy cheeks. Seven fingers. Light that came from nowhere. A face that slowly turned into my cousin halfway through a clip.

The good news is that the tells are predictable. Each has a cause, and each cause has a fix. "Not looking AI" doesn't mean hiding that you used AI (we'll get to labels at the end). It means the work looks crafted instead of default.

The 60-second tell scan

Before you fix anything, learn to see it. For every output:

  1. Look at it at full size, then at phone size. Some tells only show up small (light), some only big (skin, text).
  2. Check, in order: skin → hands → teeth → any text → light direction → background edges.
  3. For video, scrub frame by frame through the first and last second, and anywhere something passes in front of a face.
  4. Flip the image horizontally. A fresh view makes asymmetry and odd anatomy jump out.

Tell 1 — Plastic skin

What it looks like: Poreless, waxy, airbrushed skin. Uniform shine on forehead and nose. Faces that look like a phone-filter ad.

What causes it: Image models drift toward "average beautiful," and words like beautiful, flawless, perfect, stunning push them further. Heavy upscaling and beauty filters smooth what little texture was left. Too-soft, frontal light hides the micro-shadows that make pores visible.

The fix: 1. Delete flattery words from your prompt. Describe the camera and the light, not the beauty. 2. Ask for texture explicitly: pores, fine lines, peach fuzz, slight redness, uneven tone. 3. Use side or window light so texture casts tiny shadows. 4. Upscale with a low "creativity" or "detail invention" setting, or skip upscaling on faces. 5. Add light film grain in your editor. Grain gives the eye texture to read.

Prompt
Candid portrait of [SUBJECT], natural skin with visible pores, fine lines around the eyes, faint freckles, slight redness on the nose and cheeks, a few flyaway hairs, no makeup retouching. Soft window light from the left creating gentle shadows on the skin, slight overexposure on the window side. Shot on a 50mm lens at f/2.8, subtle film grain, true-to-life color, not airbrushed, not glossy.
Prompt
Regenerate only the selected face area. Keep the same person, same expression, same angle and same light. Replace the smooth plastic skin with realistic skin texture: visible pores, fine lines, natural uneven tone, a little shine only on the nose bridge. Do not change facial features.

Fix it: If one image is perfect except the skin, don't regenerate the whole thing. Use your tool's region edit (often called inpaint, vary region or generative fill) on the face only, with the prompt above.

Tell 2 — Hands, teeth and text

What it looks like: Extra or fused fingers, hands that melt into objects, a perfect row of identical "piano key" teeth, and signs or labels with gibberish letters.

What causes it: Hands and teeth are small, repetitive structures with huge variation across poses. Text is a precise pattern that image models approximate as "text-shaped." The more of these the frame contains, and the smaller they are, the more likely one fails.

The fix: 1. Simplify the pose. Ask for hands holding one clear object, relaxed at the side, or in pockets. Avoid interlaced fingers and hands near faces. 2. Close the mouth or soften the smile. "Closed-lip smile" or "slight smile, lips together" removes the teeth problem entirely. 3. Never generate important text in the image. Ask for a blank sign, label or screen, then add text in your editor. 4. Crop it out. A tighter frame that loses a bad hand is a legitimate fix. 5. Regenerate the region for hands and teeth when the rest of the image is a keeper.

Prompt
Portrait of [SUBJECT] with a relaxed closed-lip smile, one hand resting naturally on [a coffee cup] with all five fingers clearly visible and separated, the other hand out of frame. 50mm lens, waist-up framing, soft daylight, natural skin texture, realistic anatomy.
Prompt
Regenerate only the selected hand. A natural relaxed human hand with exactly five fingers, correct knuckles and fingernails, thumb on the near side, gently holding [OBJECT]. Match the skin tone, lighting direction and sharpness of the rest of the image.
Prompt
[SCENE DESCRIPTION] with a completely blank [shop sign / product label / phone screen], clean surface ready for text to be added later, no letters, no numbers, no symbols, no logos anywhere in the image.

Tell 3 — Too-perfect light

What it looks like: Everyone is evenly lit from every side. No shadows under the chin. A golden glow with no visible source. A face lit from the left in a room where the window is on the right.

What causes it: Without lighting instructions, models default to flattering, shadowless "beauty light." They don't simulate physics; they average many images. So light direction, color and source often disagree with the scene.

The fix: 1. Name one key light and its direction. "Window light from the left," "single overhead bulb," "low sun behind the subject." 2. Name a motivated source the viewer can see or infer: a lamp, a window, a screen, a neon sign. 3. Allow imperfection: a blown-out window, a deep shadow side, mixed color temperatures. 4. Match light across shots. In a sequence, copy the same light sentence into every prompt. 5. Grade it in the editor: pull highlights down slightly and add a gentle vignette.

Prompt
[SUBJECT AND SETTING]. Lit only by [a single tungsten desk lamp] on the right side of the frame: warm light on the right half of the face, the left half falling into deep shadow, visible light falloff on the wall behind. Cool, dim blue ambient light from a window out of frame. Practical lamp visible in the shot. Realistic contrast, imperfect exposure, no fill light, no rim glow.

Tell 4 — Face drift

What it looks like: Your character's face changes between images, or slowly morphs during a clip. Eyes widen, the jaw softens, they get younger or more "generic."

What causes it: Every new generation re-imagines the face from the prompt and reference. Small inconsistencies (angle, lighting, a vague description) add up. In video, the model invents each new frame from the previous ones, so errors compound over time, especially when the face turns, gets small in frame, or is briefly covered.

The fix: 1. Always use a reference. A clean, front-facing, evenly lit reference image (Chapter 3) beats any description. 2. Repeat the identity anchors in every prompt: age, face shape, eye color, hairline, one distinctive mark. 3. Keep the face big. Faces under about a fifth of the frame drift most. 4. Shorter clips. Generate 3–5 seconds and cut before the drift starts. 5. Start every shot from a still you approved. Never chain the last frame of a drifted clip into the next one. 6. Avoid big head turns and hands crossing the face unless the shot needs them.

Prompt
Same person as the reference image: [AGE]-year-old [DESCRIPTION], [FACE SHAPE] face, [EYE COLOR] eyes, [HAIR: color, length, parting], [ONE DISTINCTIVE DETAIL, e.g. small scar on left eyebrow]. Keep identity exactly the same as the reference. [NEW SCENE, POSE AND LIGHT]. Face clearly visible and large in frame, 85mm lens.
Prompt
Subtle motion only. The person blinks naturally and breathes, a slight smile forms, head stays facing the camera with less than 10 degrees of movement. No hands near the face. Camera static. Face, hairline and features must stay identical to the first frame for the entire clip. Duration 4 seconds.

Tell 5 — Video morphing

What it looks like: Objects melt, a mug becomes a bowl, a door handle slides across the door, extra limbs appear in a crowd, backgrounds swim, people pass through each other.

What causes it: Video models are predicting plausible motion, not tracking real objects in 3D. Complex prompts (many moving things, fast camera plus fast subject motion), long durations, occlusion and fine repeating patterns all raise the odds of morphing.

The fix: 1. One motion per shot. Either the camera moves or the subject does. Rarely both. 2. Describe slow, physical motion. "Slow dolly-in," "turns her head slowly." Vague words like "dynamic" invite chaos. 3. Tell the model what must not change: "the mug shape stays rigid," "no new people appear." 4. Simplify the background. Fewer people and fewer patterns mean fewer things to melt. 5. Cut earlier. Most clips are cleanest at the start. Use the first 1.5–3 seconds and cut on action into the next shot. 6. Hide seams with cutaways: a detail shot, a cover cut, a beat cut (Chapter 7).

Prompt
Camera static. [SUBJECT] slowly lifts [OBJECT] from the table and brings it toward the camera. Only the hand and the object move. The object keeps exactly the same shape, size and color throughout, completely rigid, no warping. Background stays still, no new people or objects appear. Natural speed, no slow motion. Duration 3 seconds.
Prompt
Slow, smooth dolly-in toward [SUBJECT], who stays still except for natural breathing and one blink. All architecture and furniture remain straight and rigid, no bending lines, no flickering textures. Even, steady lighting with no flicker. Duration 4 seconds.

Pro tip: Regenerate the same shot 3–4 times and pick the cleanest one. Then trim it. Cheaper than trying to rescue a melted clip in the edit.

Tell 6 — Robotic captions and voice

What it looks like and sounds like: Captions in perfect, stiff sentences ("Unlock your potential with this amazing transformation!"). A voiceover with even pacing, no breaths, wrong stress on words, and a tone that never changes.

What causes it: Text generators default to polished marketing language. Text-to-speech defaults to neutral delivery and reads punctuation literally. Nobody talks the way a press release reads.

The fix (captions): 1. Write how you'd text a friend. Contractions, fragments, one idea per line. 2. Cut every "unlock," "elevate," "game-changer," "dive in." 3. Keep on-screen lines to 2–5 words. 4. Proofread auto captions yourself; they mishear names and invented words.

The fix (voice): 1. Write the script for the ear: short sentences, natural fillers in moderation ("okay, so"), pauses marked with line breaks or ellipses. 2. Generate 2–3 takes and splice the best lines. 3. Add room tone or very low music under the voice so it doesn't float in silence. 4. Better still: record yourself on your phone in a quiet room with soft furnishings. A real voice with a small stumble beats a perfect synthetic one.

Prompt
Rewrite this caption so it sounds like a real person talking to a friend, not an ad. Use contractions, short fragments, and plain words. No exclamation marks, no words like "unlock", "elevate", "game-changer", "stunning" or "transform". Maximum 3 short lines, each under 8 words. Keep the meaning:
[PASTE YOUR CAPTION]
Prompt
Rewrite this voiceover script for a 15-second vertical video so it sounds natural when spoken aloud. Short sentences. One or two natural pauses marked with "...". One casual opener like "okay, so" or "here's the thing". Emphasis on [KEY WORD]. No marketing clichés. Read time under 15 seconds:
[PASTE YOUR SCRIPT]

Ethics: Only clone or synthesize a voice that is yours or that you have explicit permission to use. Never imitate a real person's voice.

Platform AI labels and honest disclosure

Making work that doesn't look fake is craft. Pretending it isn't AI is something else. I'm openly AI-made, and it hasn't hurt me once. Here's where things stand as of late 2026. Rules shift, so check each platform's help center before you post.

Meta (Instagram, Facebook, Threads)

  • Meta shows an "AI info" label (it replaced the older "Made with AI" tag in 2024).
  • It can be applied automatically when your file carries industry-standard AI metadata such as C2PA Content Credentials or IPTC data, which many generators embed.
  • If you post photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created or altered, you're expected to disclose it using Meta's built-in AI disclosure option when posting. Meta may apply penalties when that's skipped.
  • Ads have their own disclosure rules, and social issue, election and political ads have stricter ones.

TikTok

  • Creators can turn on the "AI-generated content" label from the post screen's more-options settings.
  • Labeling is expected when content shows realistic people or scenes that viewers could mistake for real. Clearly stylized or cartoon content generally isn't the target.
  • TikTok also reads C2PA Content Credentials and can label content automatically on upload.
  • Ads use a separate AI-content disclosure in TikTok Ads Manager.
  • Undisclosed realistic AI content can be labeled, removed or lead to account restrictions.

YouTube (including Shorts)

  • Creators must disclose realistic altered or synthetic content in YouTube Studio when uploading.
  • On Shorts the label appears as an overlay on the video; on long-form, below the player.
  • As of a May 2026 update, YouTube can also apply the label automatically when its systems detect significant photorealistic AI use, and some labels are permanent (for example, content made with YouTube's own AI tools or with C2PA metadata showing it's fully generated).
  • YouTube says the label itself doesn't change recommendations or monetization.

My simple rule

If a reasonable viewer could believe it was filmed or photographed for real, label it. If it's obviously stylized, labeling is still a nice courtesy. Never remove or strip metadata to dodge a label.

Ethics: Disclosure is about trust, not shame. Label realistic AI content, use only faces and voices you have permission to use, and never make content that puts words in a real person's mouth.
