# Chapter 2 — Prompting like a director

*Stop describing pictures. Start directing shots: subject, action, light, lens and mood, one decision at a time.*

## What you'll make

- A reusable eight-part prompt template you'll use for every image in this book
- Three versions of the same portrait that differ only in lens, so you *see* what a lens does
- A structured JSON prompt for a still and another for a video shot
- A product image built from your own reference photo
- A small "prompt log" that shows exactly which change made your image better

---

Here's a secret from someone made of prompts: AI models don't know what "beautiful" or "professional" means. They know what *85mm lens, window light from the left, shallow depth of field* means, because millions of real photos were captioned with words like those.

The fake look comes from vague prompts. When you leave a decision blank, the model fills it with the most average, most polished, most "AI" choice. Directors don't leave decisions blank.

## Prompt anatomy: the eight decisions

Every strong image prompt answers eight questions. Not all of them every time, but you should *choose* to skip one, not forget it.

| # | Decision | The question it answers | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Subject** | Who or what, specifically? | "a woman" | "a 60-year-old beekeeper with sun-weathered skin and silver braids" |
| 2 | **Action** | What are they doing right now? | "posing" | "lifting a honeycomb frame, squinting at it" |
| 3 | **Setting** | Where, and what's around? | "outside" | "an overgrown hillside apiary, wooden hives, wildflowers" |
| 4 | **Light** | Where does light come from, what color, how hard? | "nice lighting" | "low evening sun from behind, warm rim light, soft fill from a white hive" |
| 5 | **Camera / lens** | Where's the camera and what's on it? | "close-up" | "85mm lens at chest height, f/2, shallow depth of field" |
| 6 | **Film look** | What does the medium look like? | "high quality" | "muted color negative film look, fine grain, slight halation" |
| 7 | **Composition** | How is the frame arranged? | (nothing) | "subject on left third, hives fading into blur on the right" |
| 8 | **Mood** | What should it feel like? | "amazing" | "quiet, patient, end-of-summer calm" |

Notice what's missing from the strong column: "stunning," "masterpiece," "8K," "ultra-realistic." Those words don't describe a photo. They describe an ad for a photo, and they push the model toward that glossy look you're trying to escape.

> **Fix it:** If your images look like stock photos, delete every adjective that praises the image ("beautiful," "perfect," "hyper-detailed") and replace it with a physical fact (a light direction, a lens, a texture).

### The order matters

Models give the most weight to what comes first. So lead with what you care about most. For a portrait, that's the subject and action. For a product shot, it's the product. For a mood piece, you can open with light.

Here's the full template. Copy it once and keep it in your notes app:

```prompt
[SUBJECT: who or what, with 2-3 specific physical details], [ACTION: what they are doing right now]. Setting: [WHERE, plus 2-3 objects in the scene]. Light: [SOURCE and DIRECTION, color temperature, hard or soft]. Camera: [LENS in mm], [HEIGHT/ANGLE], [DEPTH OF FIELD]. Look: [FILM OR MEDIUM LOOK, grain, color]. Composition: [WHERE THE SUBJECT SITS IN THE FRAME], [ASPECT RATIO]. Mood: [ONE OR TWO FEELING WORDS].
```

And here it is filled in, ready to paste:

```prompt
A 60-year-old beekeeper with sun-weathered skin, silver braids and a faded canvas apron, lifting a honeycomb frame and squinting at it closely. Setting: an overgrown hillside apiary with weathered wooden hives and tall wildflowers. Light: low evening sun from behind, warm golden rim light on her hair, soft fill bouncing off a white hive. Camera: 85mm lens at chest height, f/2, shallow depth of field. Look: muted color negative film look, fine grain, slight halation around highlights. Composition: subject on the left third, hives fading into soft blur on the right, vertical 4:5. Mood: quiet, patient, end-of-summer calm.
```

Run it. Then run this lazy version: *"beautiful photo of an old beekeeper, ultra realistic, 8K."* Compare. That gap is what this chapter teaches.

## Camera and lens: the vocabulary that kills the AI look

You don't need to own a camera. You need to know what the words do, because the model learned from photos tagged with them.

### Focal length (the lens)

The number in millimeters controls how wide the view is and how faces look.

| Lens | What it does | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| 14–24mm (ultra-wide) | Huge field of view, stretches edges, exaggerates closeness | Big landscapes, cramped rooms, dramatic action, playful distortion |
| 35mm | Wide but natural, "you are there" | Street scenes, lifestyle, environmental portraits, handheld video |
| 50mm | Close to how your eye sees | Honest, everyday photos; the safest default |
| 85mm | Flattering compression, soft backgrounds | Portraits and headshots |
| 100mm macro | Extreme close focus | Product details, textures, jewelry, food |
| 135–200mm (telephoto) | Squeezes background close, isolates subject | Candid "caught from afar" shots, fashion, sports |
| Anamorphic | Wide cinematic frame, oval bokeh, horizontal lens flares | Movie-like video and stills |

### Camera position and movement

| Term | Meaning | Feeling it creates |
|---|---|---|
| Eye level | Camera at the subject's eyes | Neutral, honest |
| Low angle | Camera below, looking up | Power, heroism, scale |
| High angle | Camera above, looking down | Vulnerability, overview |
| Overhead / top-down | Directly above | Flat-lay products, food, maps |
| Dutch angle | Camera tilted sideways | Unease, energy |
| Over-the-shoulder | Behind one person, looking past them | Conversation, connection |
| POV | From the character's eyes | Immersion |
| Push-in / pull-out (video) | Camera moves toward or away | Push-in builds tension; pull-out reveals |
| Pan / tilt (video) | Camera turns left-right or up-down from one spot | Following action, revealing height |
| Tracking / dolly (video) | Camera travels alongside or behind the subject | Smooth, cinematic movement |
| Handheld (video) | Slight natural shake | Documentary realism |
| Orbit (video) | Camera circles the subject | Product reveals, dramatic moments |

### Shot size

Extreme wide (place is the star) → wide (full body in a setting) → medium (waist up) → close-up (face) → extreme close-up (eyes, hands, a detail). Name it every time.

### Depth of field

"Shallow depth of field" or "f/1.8" means a sharp subject and blurry background. "Deep focus" or "f/11" means everything sharp. Real phone photos often have *deeper* focus than AI defaults, which is one reason AI images look overly dreamy.

Here's the lens experiment. Run this prompt three times, changing only the lens and distance:

```prompt
Portrait of a 30-year-old desert courier with short dark curls, dusty goggles pushed up on her forehead and a sun-faded scarf, leaning against a dented motorbike. Setting: an empty two-lane road through red rock desert. Light: harsh midday sun from above, hard shadows. Camera: [24mm lens, 1 meter away / 50mm lens, 2 meters away / 135mm lens, 6 meters away], eye level. Look: natural color, slight film grain. Composition: subject centered, horizontal 3:2. Mood: tired, proud, unhurried.
```

At 24mm, her face gets slightly wider and the road feels endless. At 135mm, the cliffs crowd in behind her and she looks calmer. Same person, three stories.

## Light: the fastest way to look real

AI's default light is flat, glowing, and comes from nowhere. Real light comes from *somewhere*. When you name the source, shadows start making sense, and the brain stops screaming "fake."

### Lighting vocabulary

| Term | What it looks like | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Window light | Soft, directional, falls off across the room | Natural portraits, interiors, food |
| Golden hour | Low, warm sun, long shadows | Warm, nostalgic, outdoor portraits |
| Blue hour | Cool, even twilight after sunset | Calm, cinematic city scenes |
| Overcast | Very soft, almost shadowless | Flattering skin, product catalog |
| Hard midday sun | Strong shadows under eyes and nose | Gritty, honest, summer heat |
| Backlight / rim light | Light behind subject, glowing edges | Separation, drama, hair glow |
| Side light (split) | Half the face lit, half in shadow | Drama, mystery |
| Loop lighting | Key light slightly above and to the side, small nose shadow | Classic flattering portrait |
| Butterfly lighting | Light high and in front, small shadow under the nose | Beauty, glamour headshots |
| Practical lights | Lamps, neon, candles *inside* the scene | Moody interiors, night scenes |
| Mixed color temperature | Warm lamp plus cool window | Cinematic realism |
| Bounce / fill | Light reflected back into shadows | Softening harsh light |
| Low-key | Mostly dark, small pools of light | Thriller, luxury, mystery |
| High-key | Bright, few shadows | Clean, cheerful, commercial |

Rule of thumb: **one source, one direction, one color**, then add a second only if you need it.

```prompt
Close-up portrait of a 1970s jazz singer, an original character in her 40s with a short natural afro and gold hoop earrings, eyes closed mid-note, one hand near the microphone stand. Setting: a small smoky club, out-of-focus tables behind her. Light: a single warm spotlight from above and slightly left, hard shadows under her cheekbones, the background falling into near darkness, faint cool blue practical light from an exit sign far behind. Camera: 85mm lens, slightly low angle, f/2. Look: warm color film look, visible grain, gentle halation around the spotlight. Composition: face on the right third, vertical 4:5. Mood: intimate, late-night, a little melancholy.
```

> **Pro tip:** If skin looks plastic, the light is usually too soft and too frontal. Move the light to the side and make it a little harder. Texture lives in shadows.

## Film looks: choosing the medium

"Film look" tells the model what the *medium* looked like: color, contrast, grain. It's the difference between "a photo" and "a photo from somewhere."

I don't use brand names for film stocks or cameras in prompts. Describing the look works just as well and keeps you clear of trademarks. Useful descriptions:

- **Warm color negative film:** soft contrast, warm skin, pastel highlights, fine grain. Great for lifestyle and nostalgia.
- **Punchy slide film:** saturated color, deep blacks, crisp. Great for travel and fashion.
- **Black-and-white with heavy grain:** gritty, timeless, documentary.
- **Instant film:** soft focus, washed color, faded edges, square frame. Great for "found memory" shots.
- **Early-2000s compact digital:** on-camera flash, slight overexposure, date-stamp vibe. Great for party scenes.
- **Phone camera:** deep focus, slightly sharpened, natural HDR. Great for UGC-style ads and "real" photos.
- **Cinematic digital:** wide anamorphic frame, teal-and-warm grading, soft highlight roll-off. Great for trailer-style stills.

```prompt
A group of five friends in their 20s laughing around a kitchen table covered in pizza boxes and paper cups at 2 a.m. Light: harsh direct on-camera flash, slight red-eye, dark background behind them. Camera: compact digital camera look, 28mm, slightly tilted, one person's arm cut off at the edge of frame. Look: early-2000s compact digital photo, slightly overexposed faces, mild noise, cool white balance. Composition: messy and candid, horizontal 4:3. Mood: chaotic, warm, a night you'd remember.
```

That "imperfect" prompt will look more real than any "ultra-realistic 8K" prompt you've ever written. Imperfection is a feature.

## Composition and mood

Composition tells the model *where* things go. Without it, the model centers everything and fills the frame symmetrically, another AI tell.

Useful phrases:

- "subject on the left third," "lots of empty sky above"
- "foreground object out of focus" (a cup, a leaf, a doorframe) for depth
- "framed through a window / doorway"
- "negative space on the right for text" (essential for ads)
- "cropped tightly, part of the head out of frame" for editorial energy

Mood is your last line, and it's a tiebreaker. When the model has to choose between two options, "lonely" and "cozy" will push it in very different directions. Keep it to one or two words.

## Structured prompts: JSON for control freaks (like me)

When prompts get long, sentences get tangled. A model can't always tell that "warm" was about the light and not the jacket. Structured prompts fix this by putting each decision in its own labeled box.

JSON is just a format for labeled boxes: `"label": "value"`, separated by commas, wrapped in curly braces. You don't need to code. You need to copy the shape.

As of late 2026, the Flux 2 family officially supports JSON prompts. Other tools don't promise it, but the labels are plain English, so most chat-style tools can read them. Test it in yours, and if a tool ever chokes on it, paste the same content as plain sentences.

Here's a full example for a still:

```prompt
{
  "subject": {
    "description": "a desert courier, original character, woman in her early 30s",
    "details": ["short dark curls", "dusty goggles pushed up on forehead", "sun-faded olive scarf", "small scar on left eyebrow"],
    "wardrobe": "patched canvas jacket, fingerless leather gloves, worn boots"
  },
  "action": "checking a paper map spread on the seat of her motorbike, one finger tracing a route",
  "setting": {
    "location": "roadside fuel stop in red rock desert",
    "props": ["dented motorbike with saddlebags", "old rusted fuel pump", "tumbleweed against a fence"],
    "time": "late afternoon"
  },
  "lighting": {
    "key": "low sun from the right, warm and hard",
    "fill": "sky light, cool and soft",
    "notes": "long shadows stretching left, dust glowing in the air"
  },
  "camera": {
    "lens": "50mm",
    "angle": "slightly low, waist height",
    "shot_size": "medium shot",
    "depth_of_field": "moderate, background recognizable but soft"
  },
  "look": {
    "medium": "warm color negative film",
    "grain": "fine",
    "color": "dusty oranges, faded teal sky, natural skin tones"
  },
  "composition": {
    "framing": "subject on right third, empty road leading away on the left",
    "aspect_ratio": "4:5"
  },
  "mood": "self-reliant, quiet focus",
  "avoid": ["text", "logos", "extra people", "glossy skin"]
}
```

Why this helps: when you want to change *only* the light, you change only the `lighting` box. Everything else stays word-for-word identical. That's the foundation of iterating like a pro (more below).

And the same idea for a video shot, which you'll use in Chapter 6:

```prompt
{
  "type": "image-to-video",
  "duration_seconds": 5,
  "first_frame": "use the uploaded image exactly",
  "subject_motion": "she folds the map, looks up at the road ahead, and exhales slowly",
  "secondary_motion": ["scarf moves gently in the wind", "dust drifts across the frame"],
  "camera": {
    "movement": "slow push-in from medium shot to medium close-up",
    "stability": "smooth, no shake"
  },
  "speed": "real-time, unhurried",
  "keep_consistent": ["face", "hair", "scarf color", "motorbike", "lighting direction"],
  "audio": "wind, distant engine ticking as it cools, no music, no dialogue"
}
```

> **Heads up:** JSON is a tool for clarity, not a magic spell. A vague value in a neat box is still vague. "lighting": "nice" won't save you.

## Reference images: show, don't tell

Words are slow at describing a face, a specific product or a color palette. A reference image does it instantly. As of late 2026, most major tools accept them:

- **Nano Banana 2** (Gemini app, Flow) can keep the likeness of up to five characters and the form of up to 14 objects in one workflow.
- **Flux 2**'s top model accepts up to 10 reference images.
- **Midjourney**'s newer models replaced older reference features with an Edit Model.
- **Seedance 2.0** accepts up to 9 images, 3 videos and 3 audio clips as references for video.

Three kinds of reference, and how to talk about each:

1. **Identity reference** (a face or a product): "Keep this exact person / product."
2. **Style reference** (a mood, palette or medium): "Match the color and light of image 2, not its content."
3. **Composition reference** (a pose or layout): "Use the pose and framing of image 3."

The key is to *label* your references so the model knows which job each image does:

```prompt
Image 1 is my product: a [PRODUCT, e.g. "hand-poured soy candle in an amber glass jar with a plain kraft label"]. Keep its exact shape, glass color, label size and proportions. Image 2 is a style reference only: match its soft, warm, late-afternoon light and muted beige palette, but do not copy any objects from it. Create a new photo: the candle, lit, on a stone bathroom ledge beside a folded linen towel and a sprig of dried lavender. Camera: 100mm macro lens, eye level with the flame, shallow depth of field. Look: natural, slightly warm, fine grain. Composition: candle on the left third, empty space on the right for text, vertical 4:5. No text, no logos added.
```

> **Ethics:** Use identity references only for faces you have the right to use: yours, or someone who gave permission. Never use a real person's face (a celebrity, an ex, a coworker) without consent, and never make anyone appear to say or do something they didn't. When posting, apply the platform's AI label where required (Meta's "AI info," TikTok's AI label, YouTube's altered or synthetic content disclosure). Chapter 3 covers using your own face safely.

> **Pro tip:** Clean references give clean results. Use a sharp, well-lit reference with a plain background. A blurry selfie in a dark bar gives the model blurry information.

## Negative instructions: say what you want instead

Some tools have a separate box for things you *don't* want. Many modern models don't, and as of late 2026, Black Forest Labs says Flux 2 doesn't support negative prompts at all.

Even where "no" works, it has a quirk: mentioning something can plant it. "No hats" puts the idea of hats in the model's head.

So use this order of preference:

1. **Replace with a positive.** Instead of "no blurry background," write "background in sharp focus." Instead of "no makeup," write "bare skin with natural texture, visible pores, light freckles."
2. **Use a short "avoid" list at the end** for things that keep sneaking in (text, logos, extra fingers, extra people). Keep it short; long lists of negatives tend to backfire.
3. **Use the tool's negative field** if it has one, and keep the main prompt clean.

Here's a prompt rewritten the positive way:

```prompt
Headshot of a 45-year-old landscape architect, an original character with salt-and-pepper stubble and rimless glasses, in a navy linen shirt with the top button open. Bare skin with natural texture, visible pores, slight redness on the nose, a few laugh lines. Background: a plain warm-grey wall in sharp focus, no decoration. Light: large soft window light from the left, gentle shadow on the right cheek. Camera: 85mm lens at eye level, f/5.6 so the whole face is crisp. Look: natural color, true-to-life skin tones, very fine grain. Composition: head and shoulders centered, vertical 4:5. Mood: approachable, competent. Avoid: text, jewelry, retouched skin.
```

## Iterating one variable at a time

This is the habit that separates people who *sometimes* get great results from people who get them on purpose.

When an image is 80% right, beginners rewrite the whole prompt. That changes ten things at once, and you never learn which change helped. Directors change one thing per take.

### The method

1. **Lock a base prompt.** Generate 2–4 images. Pick the best one as your baseline.
2. **Pick the one thing that bothers you most.** Light? Lens? Expression?
3. **Change only that line.** Everything else stays word-for-word identical (this is where JSON shines).
4. **Generate, compare to baseline, decide.** Better? It becomes the new baseline. Worse? Revert.
5. **Log it.** One line per take.

A prompt log looks like this:

| Take | What changed | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Base prompt | Good pose, skin too smooth |
| 2 | Light: soft front → hard side light | Skin texture appears, better |
| 3 | Lens: 85mm → 50mm | Too much background, reverted |
| 4 | Look: added "fine grain" | Less glossy, keep |

And here's an iteration prompt you can use inside chat-style tools (Gemini, ChatGPT) after you have an image you like:

```prompt
Keep this image exactly as it is: same person, face, pose, clothing, background, framing and lens. Change only one thing: [THE ONE CHANGE, e.g. "move the light source from the front to the left side, making it harder so shadows fall across the right cheek"]. Do not change anything else.
```

> **Fix it:** If a model keeps changing things you didn't ask for, start a fresh chat, upload your best image as the reference, and paste the one-change prompt. Long chats drift.

> **Pro tip:** Most tools let you generate several images at once. Variety between them is *noise*, not progress. Compare takes, not lucky draws.

## Putting it together: a director's prompt in five minutes

Here's the flow I use for every new image:

1. **Write one sentence of intent.** "A tired desert courier at golden hour, proud and quiet."
2. **Fill the eight decisions** using the template or the JSON.
3. **Add references** if a face, product or style must match.
4. **Generate a small batch**, pick the baseline.
5. **Iterate one variable at a time** until it's right, logging each take.

That's it. No secret words. Just decisions.

## Checklist

- [ ] My prompts answer all eight decisions, or I chose to skip one on purpose
- [ ] I deleted praise words ("stunning," "8K," "masterpiece") and replaced them with physical facts
- [ ] I named a lens, a camera height and a depth of field
- [ ] I named one light source, its direction and its color
- [ ] I used a film look described in plain words, not a brand name
- [ ] I labeled every reference image with its job (identity, style, composition)
- [ ] I wrote positives instead of long "no" lists
- [ ] I changed one variable per take and logged it

## Try it now

**The 15-minute lens and light lab.**

1. Paste the desert-courier lens experiment into your default image tool. Generate the 24mm, 50mm and 135mm versions (5 minutes).
2. Pick your favorite. Use the one-change iteration prompt to switch the light to "golden hour backlight with warm rim light on her hair" (3 minutes).
3. Run it once more, changing only the film look to "black-and-white with heavy grain" (3 minutes).
4. Write a four-line prompt log: what changed each time and whether it helped (4 minutes).

You'll finish with five images of the same character and a clear sense of what each word does. Save the best one. In Chapter 3, we'll turn her into a character you can keep in every shot.
