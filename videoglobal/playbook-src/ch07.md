# Chapter 7 — Six repeatable viral formats + the edit

*Six proven shapes for short video, the exact prompts to fill them, and how to cut it all into something people watch to the end.*

## What you'll make

- A 10–15 second party scene starring a cast of original characters you invented
- A movie-trailer cut of your own life or brand, with title cards and a "coming soon" ending
- A "me in another era" series built from your own selfie
- A cinematic product reveal and a before/after transformation for anything you sell or make
- A POV clip, plus a finished 9:16 export with captions, music and sound effects that's ready to post

Confession: I'm AI-generated, and my best clips aren't original ideas. They're *formats*: containers people already know how to watch. The brain recognizes the shape in the first second and stays to see what's inside.

You don't need a new idea every day. You need six good containers and a way to fill them fast. That's this chapter.

> **Heads up:** No format guarantees views. What a format gives you is a repeatable shape you can test, compare and improve. I'll show you what to make. The algorithm does what it does.

## How to use this chapter

Each format gives you: why it works, a shot list, image prompts (stills first, as in Chapter 6), motion prompts, hooks, and a variation formula that turns one video into a series.

Two rules apply to all six. Every character is original or is *you*: no celebrities, no famous faces, no characters from films, games or anime, no logos. And keep one character sheet per cast member (Chapter 3) so faces stay stable.

> **Ethics:** Only put a real person's face in a video if that person is you or someone who gave you clear permission. Never make a "lookalike" of a famous person. Platforms remove it, and it's not fair to them.

## Format 1 — The party scene with original characters

### Why it works
A crowd of distinct, oddly specific characters in one room is instantly watchable. Viewers play "which one am I?" and tag friends. It also builds a recurring cast you can reuse forever.

### Shot list
1. Wide establishing shot: the room, the lights, the crowd (2 s)
2. Character A makes an entrance (1.5 s)
3. Character B reacts, close-up (1 s)
4. Group moment: toast, dance move or a shared laugh (2 s)
5. Detail shot: hands, drinks, confetti, a dropped shoe (1 s)
6. Final group pose to camera (2 s)

### Image prompts

Start with a cast sheet so the faces are locked before you shoot anything.

```prompt
Character reference sheet, 5 original characters standing side by side on a plain light-gray background, full body, even soft studio light, each clearly labeled with a small number 1–5 at their feet.
1: [a retired 1970s disco roller-skater in her 60s, silver bob, gold jumpsuit]
2: [a shy botanist in his 30s, round glasses, green cardigan, holding a tiny cactus]
3: [a confident desert courier in her 20s, braided hair, dusty leather jacket]
4: [a tall night-shift baker in his 40s, flour on his forearms, white apron over a suit]
5: [a teenage chess prodigy, oversized blazer, serious expression]
Natural skin texture, realistic proportions, photographic, 35mm lens, no text other than the numbers, no logos.
```

```prompt
Wide shot of a crowded rooftop party at night, string lights and warm tungsten lamps, light haze in the air, 9:16 vertical frame. In the foreground, [CHARACTER 1 from the reference sheet] laughing mid-sentence, [CHARACTER 2] holding a drink awkwardly beside her, [CHARACTER 3] leaning on the railing. City lights softly out of focus behind them. Shot on a 28mm lens at eye level, slight motion blur on dancers in the background, natural skin texture, candid documentary feel, no logos, no text.
```

For the close-up, reuse the same prompt with "close-up of [CHARACTER 4], 85mm lens, shallow depth of field".

### Motion prompts

```prompt
Shot 1: Slow push-in through the crowd toward the railing, people dancing and talking naturally, string lights swaying slightly in the breeze. Camera handheld with gentle sway. Duration 3 seconds. Keep all faces stable, no new people appearing.
Shot 2: [CHARACTER 1] steps into frame from the left, spins once and lands a pose, skirt and hair moving naturally. Camera static at waist height. Duration 2 seconds.
Shot 3: [CHARACTER 4] raises his eyebrows, then breaks into a small laugh. Subtle head movement only. Camera static, close-up. Duration 2 seconds.
Shot 6: All five characters lean in together and look straight at the camera, then raise their glasses. Camera slowly pulls back. Duration 3 seconds.
```

Generate each shot separately from its own still. A 3-second clip trimmed to 1.5 s usually beats a 1.5-second generation.

### Hooks
- "Which one are you at the party?"
- "Every friend group has these 5 people"
- "POV: you arrived late and these are the only people left"

### Variation formula
**[Setting] + [5 archetypes from one theme] + [one shared action]**

- Office holiday party + 5 coworker archetypes + secret-santa reveal
- Wedding reception + 5 guest archetypes + bouquet toss
- High-school reunion + 5 "where are they now" archetypes + group photo

Keep the same cast for three episodes before you invent a new one. Recurring faces build recognition.

## Format 2 — The movie-trailer of your life or brand

### Why it works
Trailer grammar (black screen, low boom, title card, quick cuts, a hard stop) makes the ordinary feel epic. The contrast between "big cinema" and "small life" is the joke and the charm.

### Shot list
1. Black screen + one line of title text (1 s)
2. Moody wide: the hero in their everyday setting (2 s)
3. Three fast detail cuts: hands at work, a clock, a door (0.7 s each)
4. Hero turns to camera, intense (1.5 s)
5. Title card with your name or brand (1.5 s)
6. Comedic or quiet button: one last small moment after the title (1.5 s)

### Image prompts

```prompt
Cinematic film still, 9:16 vertical. [YOU, matching the reference photo] standing alone in [a small ceramics studio at dawn], cold blue window light from the right, a single warm practical lamp behind, dust floating in the light beams. Anamorphic lens look, subtle film grain, teal-and-amber color grade, low camera angle, serious expression. Natural skin texture, no text, no logos.
```

```prompt
Extreme close-up detail shot for a movie trailer: [hands trimming the rim of a clay bowl on a spinning pottery wheel], water droplets catching backlight, shallow depth of field, 100mm macro lens, high contrast, dark background, film grain, 9:16 vertical, no text.
```

```prompt
Minimal movie title card, 9:16 vertical, solid near-black background with soft smoke drifting at the bottom, centered title text "[YOUR TITLE]" in a wide-spaced condensed sans-serif, small subtitle underneath "[A STORY ABOUT ONE STUBBORN POTTER]", subtle metallic sheen, no logos, nothing else in frame.
```

Text inside images still fails often. If the title comes out misspelled, generate the background only and add the text in your editor (that's usually the better route anyway).

### Motion prompts

```prompt
Slow dolly-in toward [the subject] standing still, dust particles drifting through the light beams, the lamp flickering slightly. Subject breathes and blinks once, then slowly turns their head toward the camera. No other movement. Cinematic, 24fps feel. Duration 4 seconds.
```

```prompt
Macro shot: the pottery wheel spins steadily, hands gently press the clay rim, a few water droplets fly off and catch the light. Camera static. Duration 3 seconds. Keep fingers anatomically correct, five fingers per hand.
```

### Hooks
- "If my [job/week/business] was a movie"
- "Coming soon to absolutely no theaters"
- "Based on a true story. Mine."

### Variation formula
**[Genre] trailer of [ordinary routine]**

Swap the genre and keep the routine: action trailer of your morning coffee, horror trailer of opening your inbox, nature documentary of your team meeting, space epic of your delivery route. Same footage logic, new color grade and music each time.

## Format 3 — "Me in another era"

### Why it works
People love seeing a familiar face in an unfamiliar time. It's identity play. A series ("me in 12 decades") also gives viewers a reason to follow for the next one.

### Shot list
1. Your real selfie, today (1 s)
2. Flash transition
3. You in Era 1, portrait (1.5 s)
4. You in Era 2 (1.5 s), Era 3 (1.5 s), Era 4 (1.5 s)
5. Final: a slow-motion "hero" shot in your favorite era (2–3 s)

### Image prompts

Upload your own front-facing selfie as the reference (Chapter 3 covers getting a clean one).

```prompt
Portrait photograph of the person in the reference image, same face, same eye shape, same nose and jawline, as [a 1920s jazz club singer], [finger-waved short hair, beaded dress, long pearl necklace], standing in front of a velvet curtain. Lit with a single hard spotlight from above, period-accurate black-and-white film look with slight halation and grain, 50mm lens, waist-up framing, 9:16 vertical. Natural skin texture, no modern objects, no text.
```

```prompt
Portrait photograph of the person in the reference image, keep identity exactly the same, as [a 1980s aerobics instructor] in [a neon-lit gym], [bright headband, high-cut leotard, leg warmers], mid-laugh. Shot on 35mm color film with saturated colors, slight overexposure and soft flash, 9:16 vertical, waist-up. Natural skin with visible texture, period-accurate details, no logos, no text.
```

### Motion prompts

```prompt
The person turns their head slowly from profile to face the camera and gives a small knowing smile. Hair and earrings move naturally. Camera locked off. Keep the face identical to the first frame throughout. Duration 3 seconds.
```

> **Pro tip:** Era shots drift toward "generic period face" fast. Keep each motion short, keep the face large in frame, and use the cleanest first frame you have. Chapter 8 covers face drift in detail.

### Hooks
- "Me in every decade since 1900"
- "Which era should I have been born in?"
- "I asked AI to put me in the 1920s. Be honest."

### Variation formula
**Me as [job] in [decade], [place]**

Keep the job fixed and change the decade ("me as a nurse in 1890, 1940, 1990, 2090"), or keep the decade fixed and change the job ("me in the 1960s as a pilot, a chef, a rock drummer"). Both give you a ready-made series.

> **Ethics:** Era portraits are for your own face or someone who said yes. Don't put a coworker, ex, or stranger into one without asking.

## Format 4 — Cinematic product reveal

### Why it works
Reveals create a tiny open loop: something is hidden, then shown. Slow, glossy motion signals "premium" even for a $12 candle. It also gives small businesses ad-ready footage without a studio.

### Shot list
1. Darkness, a sliver of light, a hint of shape (1.5 s)
2. Texture macro: surface, material, droplets (1 s)
3. Light sweep across the product (1.5 s)
4. Full hero shot, centered, clean (2 s)
5. In-use moment: a hand picks it up (1.5 s)
6. Product + one line of text (2 s)

### Image prompts

Start from a real photo of *your* product as reference (Chapter 5) so the shape and label stay honest.

```prompt
Product hero shot of [a matte-black ceramic coffee mug with a speckled glaze] from the reference image, centered on a [dark wet slate surface], dramatic low-key lighting, a single strip softbox from the back left creating a clean rim highlight, soft fog at the base, steam rising from the cup. 100mm macro lens, shallow depth of field, 9:16 vertical, high-end commercial photography, accurate product shape and color, no extra text, no logos except the product's own.
```

### Motion prompts

```prompt
Start in near darkness. A thin beam of light slowly sweeps from left to right across [the product], revealing its shape and surface texture. Steam rises gently. Camera performs a slow orbit of about 20 degrees to the right. Product shape must stay exactly the same, no warping. Duration 4 seconds.
```

```prompt
A hand enters from the right, lifts [the product] off the surface and raises it out of frame. Fog swirls where it was. Camera static, slight slow-motion feel. Keep the product shape rigid. Duration 3 seconds.
```

### Hooks
- "This is a [$X] [product]. Watch it again."
- "We filmed our [product] like a perfume ad"
- "Small shop, big reveal"

### Variation formula
**[Product] + [one element] + [one surface]**

Elements: smoke, water splash, falling petals, ice, sand, sparks. Surfaces: wet slate, mirror, raw linen, brushed steel, moss. Five elements × five surfaces = 25 reveals from one product photo.

> **Heads up:** The product in the video must match the product people receive. Never let AI "upgrade" the material, size or finish. That's a refund (and a trust problem) waiting to happen.

## Format 5 — Before/after transformation

### Why it works
Transformation is the oldest story there is. The brain wants to see the gap close. A clean wipe or a snap cut on the beat delivers that payoff in one frame.

### Shot list
1. "Before" state, flat and honest (1.5 s)
2. Transition: hand swipe, snap, wipe, spin (0.5 s)
3. "After" state, same framing (2 s)
4. Slow detail pan across the "after" (2 s)
5. Side-by-side split (2 s)

### Image prompts

Keep camera angle, lens and framing identical. That's what sells the change.

```prompt
Photo of [a cluttered, dim home office corner: messy desk, tangled cables, a bare bulb], shot straight on at eye level, 24mm lens, flat overcast window light, 9:16 vertical, realistic, slightly underexposed, no people, no text, no logos.
```

```prompt
The exact same corner from the same camera position and lens: [the home office redesigned: clean oak desk, cable tray, a warm desk lamp, a trailing plant, framed abstract print], soft warm morning light, 24mm lens, eye level, 9:16 vertical, realistic interior photography, identical walls, window and floor as the first image, no text, no logos.
```

### Motion prompts

```prompt
Camera static. A hand swipes across the lens from left to right, briefly covering the whole frame. Duration 1 second. [Generate from the "before" still; you will cut to the "after" exactly at the moment the hand covers the lens.]
```

```prompt
Slow lateral slide from left to right across [the redesigned office], the desk lamp glows, plant leaves move slightly. Keep all furniture shapes rigid, no warping. Duration 4 seconds.
```

### Hooks
- "I gave AI my worst [room/logo/photo]. Here's what came back."
- "Before / after. Wait for it."
- "Same corner. Same budget. Different light."

### Variation formula
**Same frame, new [season / era / style / budget]**

Your shop in winter vs summer. Your brand logo as 1970s, 1990s, today. A sketch → a finished render. A rainy street → the same street at golden hour.

> **Ethics:** Use before/after for design, mood, styling and concepts. Don't use AI "after" images to show results a real product or service can't deliver (skin, weight, hair, health). That's misleading, and ad platforms treat it seriously.

## Format 6 — POV

### Why it works
"POV" puts the viewer inside the story. It's personal and relatable, and it's cheap: one location, one character, a camera that *is* the viewer.

### Shot list
1. First-person view, hands visible, the situation is clear (1.5 s)
2. The character in front of "you" reacts (1.5 s)
3. A detail that escalates the situation (1 s)
4. The twist (1.5 s)
5. Reaction freeze or a look straight into the lens (1.5 s)

### Image prompts

```prompt
First-person point-of-view photo, the viewer's own hands visible at the bottom of the frame holding [a paper boarding pass], standing at [a small rainy-night train platform], an original character, [an elderly station master with a lantern and a long gray coat], looking directly at the viewer with a puzzled expression. Wide 18mm lens at chest height, wet reflections, cool blue light with a warm lantern glow, 9:16 vertical, cinematic realism, natural skin texture, no text, no logos.
```

### Motion prompts

```prompt
First-person camera, slight handheld walking motion forward. The viewer's hands lower the boarding pass. [The station master] raises the lantern, tilts his head, then slowly shakes it as if to say no. Rain falls steadily. Duration 4 seconds. Keep hands with five fingers each and the character's face stable.
```

### Hooks
- "POV: you're the [only customer / new hire / last passenger]…"
- "POV: you found the shop that isn't on any map"

### Variation formula
**POV: you are [role] and [unexpected thing happens]**

Write ten roles ("the new apprentice", "the last guest", "the person who opened the wrong door") and ten twists. Combine. That's 100 ideas.

## The edit

Generated clips are ingredients. The edit makes them a video. I'll stay tool-light, because editors move their buttons often.

### Pick your editor

As of late 2026, these are the ones I'd start with:

| Editor | Where | Why I like it | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Instagram Edits** | Phone (iOS; Android in some regions) | Free, no watermark, auto captions, sound effects layer, made for Reels | Availability varies by country |
| **CapCut** | Phone, desktop, web | Auto captions on all three, fast templates and effects | Some advanced caption options vary by region and app version; check current plan terms |
| **DaVinci Resolve** (free) | Desktop | Pro color, sound and timeline for free | Built-in auto-subtitles are in the paid Studio version; the free version needs manual captions or a third-party plugin |

Start on your phone. Move to desktop when you're doing longer trailers or client work.

### Step 1 — Build the spine
1. Create a 9:16 project (1080 × 1920).
2. Drop every clip in shot-list order.
3. Trim each clip to its strongest moment. Most AI clips look best in their *first* 60–70%; motion gets weirder toward the end.
4. Watch it once at full speed with no sound. If you're bored, viewers will be too.

### Step 2 — Cut on action
Cut *during* movement, not before or after it. If a character starts to turn in shot A, cut to shot B while the turn is happening. The eye follows the motion and doesn't notice the cut.

Other cuts that hide seams:
- **Match cut:** a round object in shot A becomes a round object in shot B.
- **Cover cut:** a hand, a door or a dark frame fills the screen, and you cut behind it (that's the before/after trick).
- **Beat cut:** cut on the drum hit. Snap the playhead to the beat marker if your editor offers one.

### Step 3 — Set the rhythm
- **First 3 seconds:** cut every 1–2 seconds. The hook needs pace.
- **Middle:** let your best shot breathe for 2–3 seconds.
- **End:** one held shot, then out. Don't fade to black for five seconds; people swipe.

A 6-shot video usually lands between 8 and 15 seconds. That's a good target while you're learning.

### Step 4 — Captions people actually read
1. Use your editor's auto captions for any voice, then **read every line**. Auto captions misspell names and invented words constantly.
2. Keep each caption to 2–5 words on screen at a time.
3. One bold, clean sans-serif font. White text with a soft dark shadow or a semi-transparent box behind it.
4. Highlight one keyword per line in a single accent color. Not three colors. One.
5. Put the hook text on screen in the first frame. Many people watch on mute.

### Step 5 — Music and sound effects
- Pick music *before* fine-trimming, so you can cut to its beats.
- Use music from the platform's own library or a royalty-free library you have a license for. Don't use commercial songs you don't have rights to in ads.
- Add 3–6 sound effects: a whoosh on transitions, a bass hit on the title card, a click on the product, ambient crowd for the party. Sound is half of what makes AI footage feel real.
- Duck the music under any voice (around −12 to −18 dB lower than the voice is a good starting point, then trust your ears).

### Step 6 — Respect the 9:16 safe zones
On a 1080 × 1920 frame, the app covers parts of your video with buttons, your username and the caption. A September 2026 Reels spec guide puts it roughly at:

- **Top:** keep about 270 px clear (about 14%)
- **Bottom:** keep about 670 px clear (about 35%), more on the right where the action buttons sit
- **Sides:** keep about 65 px clear (about 6%)

In practice: keep faces, product and caption text in the center band. No guide overlay in your editor? Put a screenshot of the app's interface on a top layer while editing, then delete it before export.

### Step 7 — Export
- **Resolution:** 1080 × 1920
- **Format:** MP4, H.264 video, AAC audio
- **Frame rate:** 30 fps (use 24 or 25 only if your whole project was built that way; don't mix)
- Watch the exported file on your phone, full screen, once with sound and once without, before posting.

### The "comment a keyword to get the prompt" loop

You've seen it: "Comment ERA and I'll send you the prompt." Here's how it works, neutrally.

1. You post a video and say what people get if they comment a word.
2. A comment-to-DM automation tool connected through Meta's official API (ManyChat is a widely used one; there are others) replies to the comment and sends the commenter a private message with the promised thing.
3. The comments signal interest; the DM starts a direct conversation you can continue.

What to know before you use it:
- **Deliver what you promised, immediately.** The first message should contain the prompt, not a sales pitch.
- **Meta has demoted "engagement bait" since 2017**, meaning posts that ask for comments or likes just to boost reach. A keyword that delivers something real is a different thing from "comment YES if you agree," but don't use the loop on every post.
- **Only use tools that connect through Meta's official API.** Unofficial bots can put your account at risk.
- **One keyword per post**, short and easy to spell, and the free thing should be useful on its own: the full prompt, not a teaser.

## Try it now

**15 minutes: one "me in another era" triptych.**

1. (3 min) Take a front-facing selfie by a window.
2. (6 min) Run the 1920s prompt from Format 3 with your selfie as reference, then swap in two more eras.
3. (3 min) Animate the best one with the Format 3 motion prompt.
4. (3 min) In your editor: selfie (1 s) → three eras (1.5 s each) → the animated one (3 s). Add the hook "Which era should I have been born in?" on frame one, a whoosh on each cut, and export at 1080 × 1920.

You just made episode one of a series.
