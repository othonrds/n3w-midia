# Chapter 3 — One face, every shot

*How to make the same person show up in every photo and every clip, instead of a slightly different cousin each time.*

## What you'll make

- A one-page **character reference sheet**: front, three-quarter, profile, three expressions and two outfits, all clearly the same person.
- A filled-in **character card** you can paste into any tool, so you never describe your character from memory again.
- A set of five on-brand scenes with one locked face, built from either an original AI persona or your own selfies.
- A short photo-to-video test that keeps the face stable when it starts moving.

## Why faces drift

I'm AI-generated myself, so let me tell you a secret about my face: the model doesn't remember it. Every new image is a fresh guess.

When you type "a woman with brown hair and green eyes," the model reaches into millions of faces that match those words and picks one. Run it again and it picks a different one. That's drift. It isn't a bug; it's how image models work.

Three things make drift worse:

1. **Vague descriptions.** "Pretty, 30s, dark hair" fits half the planet. The model has too much freedom.
2. **Big changes in the scene.** A new angle, new light or new outfit makes the model re-solve the whole face, and small features (nose bridge, eye spacing, jawline) quietly change.
3. **Motion.** Video models generate frame by frame. A head turn or a hand passing in front of the face is where identities wobble and melt. (Chapter 8 covers the fixes for video morphing in detail.)

The cure is always the same: **give the model something to copy instead of something to imagine.** Words describe; reference images anchor. You'll use both.

> **Heads up:** No tool gives you a pixel-identical face every time. The goal is "clearly the same person" to a viewer scrolling at normal speed. That bar is very reachable.

## The identity stack: three layers that lock a face

Think of consistency as three layers. Each one you add makes drift less likely.

| Layer | What it is | When it's enough |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Fixed description | The exact same identity text in every prompt | Quick tests, illustrated styles |
| 2. Reference images | One to several images of the face fed to the tool | Most photo work |
| 3. Trained or saved identity | A character the tool stores and reuses (an "element," "ID" or saved character) | Long series, video, a persona you'll post for months |

Free tools usually get you layers 1 and 2. Paid tools add layer 3. Start at the top and climb only when you need to.

## Step 1: Write the identity anchor

Before you make any image, write the face in words. Specific, physical, boring. No "beautiful," no "stunning." Those words mean nothing to a model except "make it generic."

Describe the things a sketch artist would ask about:

1. Age range and build.
2. Face shape and jawline.
3. Eyes: shape, color, spacing, brows.
4. Nose and lips.
5. Skin: tone, undertone, texture, freckles, moles, scars.
6. Hair: color, length, texture, part, hairline.
7. One or two **signature details** that are easy to spot: a gap in the teeth, a small mole on the left cheek, a silver hoop in one ear.

Signature details are your secret weapon. Viewers recognize people by small asymmetric marks far more than by "brown eyes."

Use this prompt in any chat-style AI to turn a rough idea into a tight anchor:

```prompt
Act as a casting director writing a physical description for a sketch artist.
Character idea: [ONE-LINE IDEA, e.g. "a 34-year-old ceramic artist who runs a small studio by the sea"].
Write a 60–80 word identity description covering: age, build, face shape, jawline, eye shape/color/spacing, brows, nose, lips, skin tone and texture (include natural details like pores, freckles or fine lines), hair color/length/texture/part, and exactly two small signature details that are asymmetric and easy to recognize.
Use plain physical words only. No words like beautiful, stunning, perfect or attractive. No clothing. Output one paragraph.
```

Save the result. From now on you paste it word for word. Never paraphrase it, even a little; changing "olive skin" to "tan skin" is enough to nudge the face.

> **Pro tip:** Keep the anchor at the start of your prompt. Most models weight early words more heavily than late ones.

## Step 2: Build the character reference sheet

A reference sheet is one image (or a small set) that shows the same person from several angles and with several expressions. It does two jobs: it tells you whether the face is actually consistent, and it becomes your reference image for everything after.

### 2a. Generate the hero portrait

Start with one clean, neutral front-facing portrait. This is the master copy. Every other image descends from it, so take your time and generate several until one feels like a real person.

```prompt
[IDENTITY ANCHOR]
Front-facing passport-style portrait, head and shoulders, neutral expression with relaxed mouth, looking directly into the lens.
Plain light-gray seamless background, soft even daylight from a large window on the left, no harsh shadows.
Shot on an 85mm lens at f/4, eye level, sharp focus on the eyes.
Natural skin texture with visible pores and slight unevenness, no makeup or very light makeup, no retouching.
Plain crew-neck t-shirt in [NEUTRAL COLOR]. No jewelry except [SIGNATURE ACCESSORY IF ANY].
Photorealistic, true-to-life color.
```

Pick the winner. Check the signature details. If the mole moved or vanished, regenerate.

### 2b. The turnaround

Now feed the hero portrait back in as a reference image and ask for the angles. Most current tools let you upload an image alongside your prompt; Chapter 2 covers how reference images work in general.

```prompt
Use the attached image as the identity reference. Keep the exact same person: same face shape, eye spacing, nose, lips, skin tone, hair and signature details ([LIST SIGNATURE DETAILS]).
Create a character turnaround sheet on a plain light-gray background, four views side by side in one image, evenly spaced, same scale, same lighting:
1) front view, 2) three-quarter view facing left, 3) full left profile, 4) three-quarter view facing right.
Head and shoulders, neutral expression in all four. Same plain [NEUTRAL COLOR] t-shirt.
Soft even studio light, photorealistic, natural skin texture, no stylization.
```

### 2c. The expression row

Faces drift most when they emote. A smile changes cheeks, eyes and the nose line. Lock the expressions now so you have references for them later.

```prompt
Use the attached image as the identity reference. Same exact person, same hair, same signature details ([LIST SIGNATURE DETAILS]).
One image, three head-and-shoulders portraits side by side on a plain light-gray background, front-facing, identical lighting and framing:
1) genuine wide smile showing teeth, eyes slightly crinkled,
2) thoughtful, lips closed, slight frown of concentration,
3) surprised laugh, mouth open, head tilted back slightly.
Natural skin texture, real expression lines, photorealistic, no retouching.
```

### 2d. The wardrobe row

Outfits matter more than you'd think. If your character always wears a mustard jacket, that jacket becomes part of how people recognize them, in photos and especially in video.

```prompt
Use the attached image as the identity reference. Same exact person and signature details ([LIST SIGNATURE DETAILS]).
One image, two full-body standing poses side by side on a plain light-gray background, same lighting and scale:
Left: everyday outfit — [OUTFIT A, e.g. "faded olive work jacket, cream knit sweater, dark straight jeans, brown leather boots"].
Right: dressed-up outfit — [OUTFIT B, e.g. "charcoal wool blazer, white collared shirt, black trousers, black loafers"].
Neutral relaxed posture, arms at sides, facing camera. Photorealistic, natural fabric textures and wrinkles.
```

### 2e. Assemble the sheet

Put the hero, the turnaround, the expressions and the outfits on one canvas. Any free design app works. Label each panel ("front," "3/4 L," "smile," "outfit A"). This sheet is now your single source of truth.

> **Fix it:** One view looks like a different person? Don't keep the sheet and hope. Regenerate just that view with the hero portrait as reference, then swap it in. A sheet with one wrong face will teach every future image the wrong face.

## Step 3: Using your own face (with consent)

Maybe you don't want a persona. Maybe you want *you*, in places you've never been, without booking a photographer. Great. Your selfies become the reference sheet.

Here's what to shoot. Chapter 4 goes deeper on phone technique; this is the identity set:

1. **Front, neutral.** Eye level, phone at arm's length or on a stack of books, face a window.
2. **Three-quarter left and right.** Turn your head, not your body.
3. **Both profiles.** Ask someone to help or use the self-timer.
4. **Smile with teeth, and a closed-mouth smile.**
5. **One full-length shot** so the tool learns your build.
6. **Two or three different days.** Different hair days and light teach the tool what's "you" and what's just Tuesday.

Rules that save you hours:

- No sunglasses, no hats, no hands near the face, no heavy filters.
- Recent photos only. If you've changed your hair or weight, old photos will fight new ones.
- Use the main (rear) camera when you can; front cameras often smooth skin and widen the face.
- Don't stand too close. Very close phone shots stretch the nose and shrink the ears, and the model will learn that distortion as your face.

> **Ethics:** Only use faces of people who said yes. That means you, or a person who gave you clear permission for this specific use, ideally in writing (a text message counts). Your partner, your client, your best friend: ask first, show them the results, delete if they say so.

## Step 4: Lock identity in current tools

Tools change monthly, so treat this section as "as of late 2026." The principle underneath never changes: **reference images in, same face out.** Here is what I checked was available when I wrote this.

### Free first: chat-based image editors

**Google Gemini app (the "Nano Banana" image models).** Google built its Gemini image editing to keep people and pets looking like themselves across edits, and you can upload several photos and blend them into one new scene. It's free to try, and the newer Nano Banana models accept multiple reference images in one request. Everything made or edited in the Gemini app carries a visible watermark plus Google's invisible SynthID watermark, which is a good thing: it's honest by default.

Best for: quick consistent edits of a real photo ("same person, new place, new outfit").

How to use it for identity:

1. Upload your hero portrait (or your best front selfie) plus one three-quarter view.
2. Start the prompt by naming what must not change.
3. Change one thing at a time: location first, then outfit, then expression.

```prompt
Keep the person in the attached photos exactly as they are: same face, same facial proportions, same skin tone and texture, same hair, same signature details ([LIST SIGNATURE DETAILS]). Do not beautify, slim or smooth the face.
Change only the scene: place them [SCENE, e.g. "at a wooden workbench in a sunlit pottery studio, shelves of unglazed bowls behind them"].
They are [ACTION, e.g. "looking down at a bowl in their hands, slight smile"].
Wearing [OUTFIT FROM CHARACTER CARD].
Natural window light from the right, 50mm lens, eye level, shallow depth of field. Photorealistic, candid documentary feel.
```

### Paid upgrade: image tools with reference controls

**Midjourney.** As of late 2026, V8.2 is the default model. On the V8 family, character consistency runs through the Edit model, where you can attach up to four reference images. The older Omni Reference (`--oref`, with `--ow` controlling strength) is a V7 feature, and the very old `--cref` belongs to V6. If a tutorial tells you to use `--cref`, it's out of date. Midjourney is strongest for stylized and editorial looks; for documentary realism you'll do more work.

**Higgsfield Soul ID.** This is a layer-3 tool: you train an identity once and then pick the character from a menu instead of re-uploading references. Higgsfield's own guidance asks for at least 20 photos of the same person (varied angles and expressions, at least one full-height shot, recent, no sunglasses or heavy shadows), and training takes a few minutes. Their own article says to expect "clearly the same person," not a pixel-identical face, which matches my experience with every tool. Check current pricing before you commit.

### Video: saved characters

**Kling Element Library.** Kling lets you save a character (or prop, outfit or scene) as a reusable "element": a front-facing main image plus up to three supplementary angles, with a name and description. As of its February 2026 guide, elements work with Kling's VIDEO 3.0 and 3.0 Omni models, and the Omni models can also bind a voice and build an element from a short 3–8 second video of one character. That last part is exactly why you made a turnaround: you already have the front plus three angles it asks for.

| Tool (as of late 2026) | Consistency method | Cost tier | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gemini app (Nano Banana) | Upload photos, describe the change | Free to try, paid tiers | Real-photo edits, quick scenes |
| Midjourney V8 Edit model | Up to 4 reference images | Paid | Stylized, editorial, fashion |
| Higgsfield Soul ID | Trained identity from 20+ photos | Paid | Long-running persona series |
| Kling Element Library | Saved character from 1 main + up to 3 angles | Free trial, paid | Keeping a face stable in video |

> **Pro tip:** Name your character the same thing everywhere: the element name, the file names, the character card. "Ines_v1_front.png" beats "IMG_4471.png" when you're juggling four tools.

## Step 5: The character card

This is the document you'll use more than any other in this book. One card per character. Paste it into a note app and fill it in once.

```prompt
CHARACTER CARD — [CHARACTER NAME] — version [V1]

ROLE: [one line, e.g. "AI persona; ceramic artist; warm, curious, a bit messy"]
AI PERSONA OR REAL PERSON: [AI persona / me / real person with written consent from NAME on DATE]

IDENTITY ANCHOR (paste word for word, never edit):
[60–80 WORD PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION]

SIGNATURE DETAILS (must appear in every image):
1. [e.g. small mole above left eyebrow]
2. [e.g. silver hoop in right ear only]

NEVER CHANGE: [face shape, eye color, hair color and length, skin texture, signature details]
CAN CHANGE: [outfit, location, lighting, expression, pose]

WARDROBE:
- Signature outfit: [OUTFIT A]
- Dressed-up outfit: [OUTFIT B]
- Signature color: [e.g. mustard yellow]

LOOK & LIGHT DEFAULTS: [e.g. "natural window light, 50mm, warm color grade, light film grain"]

REFERENCE FILES:
- Hero front: [FILE NAME]
- 3/4 left / 3/4 right / profile: [FILE NAMES]
- Expressions: [FILE NAME]
- Outfits: [FILE NAME]

TOOL IDS: [e.g. Kling element name, trained ID name]

DISCLOSURE: [how this character is labeled on each platform]
```

The "NEVER CHANGE / CAN CHANGE" lines are the heart of it. When you write a new prompt, you only touch the "can change" list.

## Step 6: Shoot a consistent series

Now put the character to work. Use the hero portrait plus the closest-angle reference for each shot. If the scene shows a three-quarter view, attach the three-quarter reference. Matching the angle of the reference to the angle of the shot is the single biggest consistency upgrade I know.

Here's a reusable scene template:

```prompt
Reference: attached images show [CHARACTER NAME]. Keep identity identical: [PASTE IDENTITY ANCHOR]. Signature details visible: [SIGNATURE DETAILS].
Scene: [LOCATION AND TIME OF DAY].
Action: [WHAT THEY ARE DOING, one verb].
Framing: [close-up / medium shot / full body], [camera angle], [LENS, e.g. 35mm].
Wardrobe: [OUTFIT FROM CARD].
Light: [LIGHT DEFAULTS FROM CARD].
Style: photorealistic, natural skin texture, candid, no retouching, no text, no logos.
```

Five scenes I'd make for any persona, so the feed tells a small story:

1. A close-up at their workplace.
2. A medium shot walking outside, in the signature outfit.
3. A quiet moment at home (coffee, window, book).
4. A dressed-up evening shot in outfit B.
5. A detail shot: hands at work, face partly visible.

Lay all five side by side. Squint. Do they read as one person? If one breaks, regenerate it with a closer-angle reference.

> **Fix it:** The face keeps getting "prettier" each round (smaller nose, bigger eyes, smoother skin)? That's the model drifting toward its idea of average beauty. Add "do not beautify, keep exact proportions and natural skin texture" and always re-reference the **original** hero portrait, not the latest output. Copies of copies drift.

### Group shots with two characters

Two consistent characters in one frame is harder. Give each one a card and a reference, and describe them by position.

```prompt
Two people in one photo. Keep both identities exactly as in their references.
Person on the LEFT is [CHARACTER A NAME] (first attached image): [SHORT ANCHOR A], wearing [OUTFIT A].
Person on the RIGHT is [CHARACTER B NAME] (second attached image): [SHORT ANCHOR B], wearing [OUTFIT B].
Scene: [LOCATION]. They are [SHARED ACTION, e.g. "laughing while unpacking a crate of plates"].
Medium shot, 35mm, eye level, soft daylight. Photorealistic, natural skin, no merging of features between the two people.
```

If faces swap or blend, generate each person separately in the same scene and composite, or use a tool that lets you assign references to regions.

## Step 7: Keep the same face from photo to video

Here's where most people lose their character. The photo is perfect, the video turns them into a stranger by second three.

The rule: **always start video from an image you already approved.** Don't ask a video model to invent your character from text. Give it the finished still as the first frame, and if the tool supports saved characters or elements, attach those too. Chapter 6 covers image-to-video in depth; here's the identity-specific workflow.

1. **Pick a strong first frame.** Face visible, well lit, three-quarter or front. Avoid starting on a profile; the model has to invent the other side of the face as it turns.
2. **Attach the character.** In Kling, add your saved element. In other tools, attach the hero portrait as a reference if allowed.
3. **Ask for small motion.** Subtle head turns, a smile, walking toward camera. Big spins and fast hand gestures near the face are where identity breaks.
4. **Keep clips short.** Shorter clips drift less. Stitch several short, good clips instead of one long risky one.
5. **Check the last frame.** If it still looks like your character, you can use it as the first frame of the next clip.

```prompt
Start from the attached image. Keep [CHARACTER NAME]'s face, hair, skin texture and signature details ([SIGNATURE DETAILS]) identical throughout the entire clip.
Motion: [SMALL ACTION, e.g. "she looks up from the bowl, notices the camera and gives a small warm smile"].
Camera: slow push-in, steady, no cuts.
Keep the face fully visible and unobstructed; hands stay below chin level.
Natural, realistic motion, no morphing, no change in facial features or hair length.
Duration: [SHORT DURATION supported by the tool].
```

> **Fix it:** Face morphs mid-turn? Reduce the turn ("turns head slightly toward camera" instead of "turns around"), shorten the clip, or start from the three-quarter frame instead of the profile.

### Make a "video reference" of your character

If your tool can build a character from a short clip (Kling's 3.0 Omni elements can, from 3–8 seconds), generate one clean, calm clip of your persona: front-facing, slight head movement, good light, plain background. Save that as the character. Every later clip borrows from it.

```prompt
Start from the attached front-facing portrait. Same person, identical features.
A calm 5-second identity clip: the person looks into the lens, slowly turns their head about 30 degrees to their left, then back to center, then gives a small natural smile.
Plain light-gray background, soft even studio light, static camera, head and shoulders framing.
No hand movement, no hair covering the face, no change in features. Photorealistic.
```

## Version control for faces

Characters evolve. Haircut, new season, new look. Handle it like software:

- Keep **V1** files untouched, always.
- When you change something on purpose, make **V2**: new hero portrait from V1, new card, new turnaround.
- Note the change on the card ("V2: hair cut to chin length, Oct").
- Never mix V1 and V2 references in the same prompt.

## The ethics box

> **Ethics:** Read this before you post anything.
>
> 1. **Never recreate a real person without their consent.** Not a celebrity, not a coworker, not an ex, not "someone who just looks a bit like." If a viewer could reasonably think it's a specific real person, it's off-limits unless that person said yes.
> 2. **Don't use a stranger's photo as a reference.** "Inspired by" a face from the internet is still that person's face.
> 3. **Label your AI persona.** Say it in the bio ("AI-generated creator") and use the platform labels. As of 2026: Meta has an "AI info" label and an AI disclosure control for photorealistic synthetic content; TikTok asks you to switch on its "AI-generated content" label for realistic AI people, places or events; YouTube asks you to mark "altered or synthetic" content in Studio when viewers could mistake it for real. Rules evolve, so check each platform when you upload.
> 4. **Don't let a persona pretend to be human.** I'm openly AI, and my audience likes me more for it, not less. Trust lasts longer than illusion.
> 5. **Your own face is still data.** Read a tool's privacy terms before training an identity on yourself, and delete trained IDs you no longer use.

## Try it now

**15 minutes: the three-shot identity test.**

1. (4 min) Run the casting-director prompt and save your identity anchor with two signature details.
2. (4 min) Generate the hero front portrait. Regenerate until the signature details are clearly visible.
3. (5 min) In a free tool that accepts reference images, make three scenes with the hero attached: a close-up at work, a walk outside, an evening shot in a different outfit.
4. (2 min) Put all four images side by side. Circle anything that changed (nose, brows, mole, hairline). That list is what you'll add to "NEVER CHANGE" on your character card.

If all four read as one person, you just solved the problem that makes most AI content look fake. Next chapter, we point the same skills at your own selfies.
