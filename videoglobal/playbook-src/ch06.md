# Chapter 6 — From still to motion

*Turn your best images into short, steady, cinematic clips, one shot at a time.*

## What you'll make

- Your first image-to-video clip with motion you actually chose, not motion the AI guessed
- A personal camera-move cheat sheet you can paste into any video tool
- A start-and-end-frame transition (for example, an empty table to a styled product)
- A four-shot mini sequence built from a simple shot list
- A repair plan for the two classic AI video failures: morphing and drift

Everything in Parts 1 and 2 was building to this. A great still image is the best possible starting point for video, because you've already locked the face, the product, the light and the look.

I'm AI-generated myself, and I'll be honest: video is where AI gets caught most often. A face that slowly becomes someone else. A hand with an extra finger for six frames. A coffee mug that melts into the table.

The good news: almost all of that comes from asking for too much at once. This chapter teaches you to ask for less, and get much more.

## How image-to-video actually works

Text-to-video starts from nothing and imagines everything: the character, the room, the light, the motion. Image-to-video starts from your picture and only has to imagine **what happens next**.

That's why I almost always start from an image:

| | Text-to-video | Image-to-video |
|---|---|---|
| Face and identity | Invented each time | Locked by your image |
| Product accuracy | Guessed | Starts from your real product |
| Lighting and style | Random-ish | Set by you, in the image |
| What your prompt describes | Everything | Only the motion |
| Best for | Brainstorming, abstract b-roll | Characters, products, anything that must stay consistent |

So the job of your video prompt changes. You're not describing the scene anymore. The image already does that. **You're describing movement.**

### Which tools, as of late 2026

Tools change fast, so treat this as a snapshot and check what's current before you subscribe. These are the features I verified this month:

| Tool | Image-to-video | Start + end frame | Multi-shot | Native audio |

> **Pro tip:** Start every new tool with the same test image and the same prompt. In ten minutes you'll know which one handles your kind of shot best. Principles carry over; tools come and go.

## Writing motion prompts

### Subject motion vs camera motion

Every clip has two kinds of movement, and mixing them up is the number one beginner mistake.

- **Subject motion** is what moves *inside* the scene: a person turns their head, steam rises, a candle flame flickers, a sneaker lace swings.
- **Camera motion** is how the *viewer's eye* moves: pushing closer, circling around, rising up.

If you write "the woman walks toward the camera as the camera moves forward," the AI has to solve two moving targets at once. That's where faces warp and backgrounds slide.

**My rule for beginners: one subject motion + one camera move per clip.** Later you can combine more. At first, keep it simple and you'll keep it stable.

### The motion prompt formula

Write your prompt in this order. Keep it short: 40–80 words beats a paragraph.

1. **Camera move** — one move, with speed (slow, gentle, steady)
2. **Subject action** — one clear action, with a start and an end
3. **Secondary motion** — small natural details (hair in the breeze, steam, dust in light)
4. **What stays still** — "the background remains steady," "the product does not change shape"
5. **Mood and pacing** — calm, tense, playful; real-time or slow motion

```prompt
Slow, steady push-in toward the subject. [SUBJECT, e.g. a woman in a mustard wool coat sitting at a café window] slowly lifts her cup, takes a small sip, and lowers it again while looking out the window. Light steam rises from the cup; her hair moves slightly. The background and the window frame stay steady. Her face and clothing remain consistent throughout. Calm, quiet mood, natural real-time pacing, cinematic, photorealistic.
```

```prompt
Static locked-off camera, no camera movement. [PRODUCT, e.g. a matte sage-green ceramic coffee mug] sits on a light-oak table in morning light. Thin wisps of steam rise slowly from the mug and drift to the right. A soft shadow of a leaf outside the window sways gently across the table. The mug does not move or change shape, color or size. Peaceful, slow, natural, photorealistic.
```

> **Heads up:** Don't re-describe the whole image in your motion prompt. If your image shows a red coat and your prompt says "blue coat," you've just asked the model to repaint the coat mid-clip. Describe motion, and name the subject only enough to point at it.

## Camera move vocabulary

These are the words video tools understand best. Use the exact term, add a speed, and say what it should feel like.

| Move | What it does | Feels like | Best for | Prompt wording |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Push-in** | Camera moves straight toward the subject | Focus, intimacy, "pay attention" | Faces, product reveals, emotional beats | "slow push-in toward [subject]" |
| **Pull-out** | Camera moves straight away | Revealing context, ending, loneliness | Opening a scene, final shots | "slow pull-out revealing the room" |
| **Dolly (side / tracking)** | Camera glides sideways or follows alongside | Smooth, premium, cinematic | Walking shots, product lineups | "smooth dolly left, tracking alongside [subject]" |
| **Orbit** | Camera circles around the subject | Showcase, hero moment | Products, a character's reveal | "slow 90-degree orbit around [subject], subject stays centered" |
| **Crane / jib** | Camera rises or lowers vertically | Grandeur, establishing, ending | Openers, reveals over a scene | "slow crane up revealing the [setting]" |
| **Handheld** | Small natural shakes and drift | Real, documentary, "I was there" | UGC style, POV, energetic scenes | "subtle handheld camera, gentle natural sway" |
| **Whip pan** | Very fast horizontal swing that blurs | Energy, surprise, transition | Cutting between two shots | "fast whip pan to the right with motion blur" |
| **Static (locked-off)** | No movement at all | Calm, observational, confident | Product loops, subtle life moments | "static locked-off camera, no camera movement" |

> **Fix it:** If the tool ignores your camera move, put it first in the prompt and repeat the key word once: "Slow orbit. The camera orbits slowly around the candle..." Some tools also have a separate camera-control setting; when they do, use it instead of words.

### Moves that are safest for beginners

Ranked from most stable to least:

1. Static
2. Slow push-in
3. Slow pull-out
4. Slow dolly
5. Crane
6. Orbit (beautiful, but the AI must invent the back of the subject)
7. Handheld (fine, but can amplify jitter)
8. Whip pan (great as a transition, hard as a whole clip)

Orbits deserve a warning. When the camera circles your product, the tool has to imagine sides it never saw. If you sell that product, give it a reference image of the back, or keep the orbit small (30–90 degrees).

```prompt
Slow 45-degree orbit to the right around [PRODUCT, e.g. a frosted glass skincare dropper bottle] standing on a pale travertine shelf. The bottle stays centered and perfectly still; only the camera moves. Soft morning light from the left creates a gentle moving highlight on the glass. The label and the bottle shape stay exactly the same throughout. Elegant, premium, slow pacing, shallow depth of field, photorealistic.
```

```prompt
Slow crane up starting at street level and rising to reveal [SETTING, e.g. a quiet seaside town at sunrise with pastel houses and a calm harbor]. [SUBJECT, e.g. a desert courier in a dusty linen jacket] walks away from the camera along the waterfront in the lower third of the frame. Seagulls glide in the distance, light mist over the water. Calm, hopeful, cinematic, natural real-time pacing, photorealistic.
```

## Why 5–10 second clips win

Longer clips sound better. They're usually worse.

Every second the model generates is another second where the face can drift, the hands can glitch and the physics can bend. Errors stack up over time. A 5-second clip has fewer chances to go wrong than a 15-second one.

There's also an editing reason: real films cut every few seconds. A 4-second shot of a face, cut to a 3-second detail, cut to a 5-second wide, feels cinematic. A single 15-second AI clip with nothing changing feels like a screensaver.

My defaults:

| Clip type | Length | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Product loop | 5 s | Easy to loop seamlessly, low drift |
| Character action | 5–8 s | Enough time for one complete action |
| Establishing / scenic | 6–10 s | Slow moves need time to breathe |
| Dialogue line | 5–10 s | One line per clip keeps lip-sync tight |

Generate 2–3 versions of each short clip, keep the best, and cut them together in an editor. You'll spend less and get better results than one long attempt.

## Start and end frames

Start-and-end-frame generation (Kling calls it Start & End Frames; Google's Flow calls it Frames to Video) lets you give the tool **two images** and have it create the motion between them. It's the most powerful control in image-to-video, because you decide where the clip begins *and* where it lands.

### What it's great for

- **Transformations:** an empty table becomes a styled breakfast scene
- **Reveals:** a closed box, then the product sitting on top
- **Outfit or era changes:** the same character, two looks
- **Seamless loops:** use the same image as start and end

### How to make good frame pairs

1. **Create the start frame first.** Use your best image from earlier chapters.
2. **Make the end frame by editing the start frame**, not by generating a new image. Same camera angle, same light, same character. Only change what should change.
3. **Keep the change believable for the clip length.** A 5-second clip can move a mug across a table. It can't turn day into a different city without looking like a morph.
4. **Describe the in-between in your prompt.** Tell the tool *how* to get from A to B.

```prompt
Start frame: an empty light-oak table by a window in soft morning light. End frame: the same table styled with [PRODUCT, e.g. a sage-green ceramic mug], a linen napkin and a plate of toast.

Static camera. A hand enters from the right and places the [PRODUCT] in the center of the table, then a second motion sets down the napkin and plate beside it. Objects appear only by being placed by the hand, never by fading in. Steady light, no change in camera angle. Natural real-time pacing, photorealistic.
```

```prompt
Start frame and end frame are the same image of [PRODUCT, e.g. an amber glass jar candle] on a wooden side table at dusk. Create a seamless 5-second loop: the candle flame flickers gently, a thin ribbon of smoke curls up and fades, warm light pulses very slightly on the table. Static locked-off camera. The jar, label and wax do not change. The final frame matches the first frame exactly so the clip loops smoothly.
```

> **Fix it:** If the transition between frames looks like a melt or a crossfade, the two frames are too different. Add a middle frame and generate two shorter clips (A to B, then B to C) instead of one big jump.

## Multi-shot sequences and shot lists

A single clip is a moment. A sequence is a story. As of late 2026, some tools (Kling 3.0's Multi-Shot, for example) can generate several connected shots in one go, and Veo 3.1 can extend a clip shot after shot. But I still plan every sequence on paper first. The tool follows a good plan much better than a vague wish.

### Director logic: establish, action, reaction, detail

Most short sequences, from a movie scene to a 15-second product ad, use the same four building blocks:

| Shot | Job | Typical framing | Typical move |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Establish** | Where are we? | Wide | Slow crane, pull-out or dolly |
| **Action** | What's happening? | Medium | Tracking dolly, handheld, or static |
| **Reaction** | How does someone feel about it? | Close-up on a face | Slow push-in or static |
| **Detail** | What should we notice? | Extreme close-up | Static or very slow push-in |

Order them however your story needs. Product ads often go *detail, action, establish*. Emotional stories often go *establish, action, reaction*. But having all four gives your editor options.

### A shot list template

Before you generate, write a simple table like this. Every row becomes one clip.

| # | Shot type | Framing | Camera | Action | Length |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Establish | Wide | Slow crane down | A small ceramics studio at golden hour, dust in the light | 6 s |
| 2 | Action | Medium | Slow dolly right | The potter lifts a finished mug from a shelf | 5 s |
| 3 | Reaction | Close-up | Slow push-in | The potter smiles slightly, turning the mug in the light | 5 s |
| 4 | Detail | Extreme close-up | Static | Thumb runs along the glaze; light catches the rim | 4 s |

Total: 20 seconds. Four clips, four chances to get each one right.

### Generating the shots so they match

1. **Make a start image for each shot** using the same character reference and the same lighting description (Chapter 3 shows how to keep a face consistent).
2. **Reuse a "style line"** at the end of every prompt so color and mood match: for example, "golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic."
3. **Keep clothing, props and time of day identical** across shots unless the story changes them.
4. **Generate each shot 2–3 times** and pick the best.

Here are the prompts for the four shots above, all sharing one style line:

```prompt
Shot 1 — Establish. Slow crane down from ceiling height into [SETTING, e.g. a small sunlit ceramics studio with wooden shelves of handmade mugs and bowls]. Fine dust floats in the beams of light. No people moving yet. Calm, warm, inviting. Style: golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic.
```

```prompt
Shot 2 — Action. Slow dolly right, medium shot. [CHARACTER, e.g. a potter in her 50s with gray curly hair, a canvas apron and clay on her forearms] reaches up and lifts a finished [PRODUCT, e.g. matte sage-green mug] from a wooden shelf, then holds it in both hands. Her face and clothing stay consistent; the mug keeps its exact shape and color. Style: golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic.
```

```prompt
Shot 3 — Reaction. Slow push-in, close-up on [CHARACTER]'s face. She studies the mug in her hands, then a small, proud smile appears and her eyes soften. Natural skin texture, subtle movement, no exaggerated expressions. The background stays softly blurred and steady. Style: golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic.
```

```prompt
Shot 4 — Detail. Static extreme close-up. A thumb slowly runs along the rim of the [PRODUCT, e.g. matte sage-green ceramic mug]; warm light catches the texture of the glaze. Shallow depth of field, the mug fills the frame. The mug does not change shape, color or texture. Style: golden-hour light from the left, warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic.
```

If your tool supports multi-shot in one generation, you can combine the list into one prompt. Keep each shot short and labeled:

```prompt
Multi-shot sequence, 4 shots, consistent character and lighting throughout.
Shot 1 (wide, slow crane down): [SETTING], warm golden-hour light, dust in the air.
Shot 2 (medium, slow dolly right): [CHARACTER] lifts [PRODUCT] from a shelf.
Shot 3 (close-up, slow push-in): [CHARACTER] looks at it and smiles softly.
Shot 4 (extreme close-up, static): a thumb traces the rim of [PRODUCT], light on the glaze.
Same character, same clothing, same product in every shot. Warm amber tones, soft film grain, photorealistic, calm pacing.
```

> **Pro tip:** Cut on action. End shot 2 just as the hands begin to turn the mug, and start shot 3 with the mug already turning. The viewer's eye follows the motion across the cut, and the edit feels seamless.

## Fixing morphing and drift

These are the two big AI video tells. Here's what causes them and how to fix them.

**Morphing** is when objects melt, merge or change shape: a hand fuses with a cup, a chair grows an extra leg, a sneaker becomes a slipper.

**Drift** is slower: the face gradually stops looking like your character, the outfit changes color, the background slides.

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Face changes over the clip | Clip too long, big head turn, fast camera move | Shorten to 5 s; use a slow push-in or static; avoid full profile turns |
| Hands melt into objects | Complex grip, fast hand motion | Simplify the action ("holds the mug" instead of "spins the mug"); slow it down |
| Product changes shape or label | Orbit too wide, tool redrawing hidden sides | Smaller orbit; add "product does not change"; use a start-and-end frame pair of the real product |
| Background slides or warps | Subject and camera both moving | One motion per clip; add "background remains steady" |
| Objects appear from nowhere | Prompt asks for a big change | Show how it enters (a hand places it); or use start and end frames |
| Jittery or wobbly motion | Handheld + fast action | Swap handheld for static or dolly; reduce speed words |
| Body physics looks wrong | Running, dancing, fighting | Choose smaller actions; cut around the hardest moment |

### My anti-morph prompt add-on

Paste this at the end of any motion prompt when stability matters more than drama:

```prompt
[YOUR MOTION PROMPT HERE]

Stability: smooth, slow, physically realistic motion. The subject's face, body proportions and clothing remain consistent from the first frame to the last. Hands keep five fingers and do not merge with objects. [PRODUCT, if any] keeps its exact shape, color and label. The background remains steady with no warping. No sudden changes, no morphing, no flicker.
```

> **Fix it:** Still drifting? Use the last good frame of your clip as the start frame of a new clip. This "relay" technique keeps each segment short and anchored, and you join them in the edit.

## Sound and lip-sync basics

As of late 2026, several tools generate sound with the video: Veo 3.1, Kling 3.0 and Runway's Gen-4.5 all support native audio, and Kling 3.0 can produce dialogue in several languages. That changes how you prompt.

### Three layers of sound

1. **Ambience** — the room tone: café chatter, wind, rain on a window
2. **Effects** — specific sounds tied to action: a mug set on wood, a lighter click, footsteps
3. **Voice** — dialogue or narration

Describe sound in its own sentence so it doesn't compete with your motion instructions:

```prompt
Static medium shot. [CHARACTER, e.g. a 1970s jazz singer in a velvet dress, original character] sits at a small table in a dim lounge and looks straight into the camera. She says, warmly and slowly: "[LINE, e.g. Some songs you only sing once.]" Then she smiles slightly. Lip movements match the words. Her face stays consistent.

Audio: soft lounge ambience with distant glasses clinking, a quiet upright bass in the background, her voice clear, close and warm. No music over the dialogue's start.
```

### Lip-sync tips

- **One line per clip.** Short sentences sync better than long speeches.
- **Face the camera.** Front or three-quarter angles; avoid profile and covered mouths.
- **Put the exact words in quotes** and describe the delivery (whispered, cheerful, slow).
- **Keep the camera calm.** A static shot or slow push-in during dialogue.
- **If the tool's voice isn't right,** generate the video silent, record or generate the voice separately, and use a dedicated lip-sync tool (Runway offers one) or simply cut to a detail shot while the line plays.

> **Heads up:** Native audio is new and uneven. Check every clip for mismatched lips, odd pronunciations and sound effects that land late. Often the best fix is to mute the generated audio and add your own sound in the editor (Chapter 7 covers the edit).

> **Ethics:** Only animate or give a voice to faces of people who consented — yourself, or someone who said yes. Never clone or imitate a real person's voice without permission, and never make a real person "say" something. Label AI video where platforms require it: Meta's "AI info" label, TikTok's AI-generated content label, and YouTube's altered or synthetic content disclosure.

## Checklist

- [ ] I start from a strong still image, not from text, whenever consistency matters
- [ ] Each clip has one camera move and one main subject action
- [ ] I use exact camera terms (push-in, dolly, orbit, crane, handheld, whip pan, static)
- [ ] My clips are 5–10 seconds, generated in 2–3 versions each
- [ ] I've made at least one start-and-end-frame clip with an end frame edited from the start frame
- [ ] I write a shot list (establish, action, reaction, detail) before generating a sequence
- [ ] I check every clip for face drift, hand morphing and product changes before using it
- [ ] I only animate or voice faces with consent, and I label AI video where required

## Try it now

**15 minutes: your first three-shot sequence.**

1. (2 min) Pick one still image you've made: a character or a product.
2. (3 min) Write a three-row shot list: establish (wide), action (medium), detail (close-up). One camera move per row.
3. (8 min) Generate shot 2 first with a 5-second slow push-in or static camera, using the motion formula and the anti-morph add-on. Then generate the other two the same way.
4. (2 min) Watch all three back to back. Which one drifts? Fix only that one, or note it for tomorrow. You just directed your first AI sequence.
