# Chapter 5 — Product photos without a photoshoot

*One honest phone photo in, a full catalog, lifestyle set and ad grid out.*

## What you'll make

- One clean "source photo" of your product, shot on your phone in under ten minutes
- A white-background catalog set (front, three-quarter, detail) ready for marketplaces
- Three styled lifestyle scenes that look like a real shoot in a real room
- A seasonal mini-set (autumn, holiday, summer) from the same product
- A 9-image ad grid of variations you can test, plus the right crop for every platform

Here's the thing nobody tells you about AI product photos: the AI is not the photographer. Your phone is. The AI is the set designer, the lighting crew and the prop stylist.

I'm AI-generated myself, so I know exactly where these tools cheat. With people, they invent a face. With products, they invent a label. A customer forgives a slightly odd background. They do not forgive a mug that arrives a different color than the photo.

So this chapter has one rule running through every step: **the product stays real, everything around it can be imagined.**

## The one rule: never let the AI redesign your product

When you ask an image generator to "put this candle on a marble table," it doesn't paste your candle in. It looks at your photo, understands "candle, amber jar, cream label," and redraws it. Sometimes perfectly. Sometimes with a slightly taller jar, a softer color, and a label that now says "CANDEL" in a font you never chose.

That redraw is the problem. If you sell the item, the photo is a promise about what arrives in the box.

Here's what the AI tends to change without asking:

| What drifts | What it looks like | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Label text | Letters scrambled, words invented, fine print turned to squiggles | Instantly reads as fake; can misstate ingredients or sizes |
| Shape and proportions | Mug handle thicker, bottle neck longer, sneaker toe rounder | Customer receives "a different product" |
| Color | Sage becomes mint, cream becomes white, matte becomes glossy | The number one reason for "not as pictured" returns |
| Material | Ceramic looks like plastic, glass looks like acrylic | Cheapens the product |
| Small details | Stitching, a lid's ridges, a pump's shape disappear | Detail shots stop being trustworthy |

> **Heads up:** Most marketplaces require that the main image shows the actual item being sold. Check your marketplace's image policy before using any AI-edited image as a main listing photo. Lifestyle and ad images usually have more freedom, but "accurate" is never optional.

There are two safe ways to work, and you'll use both:

1. **Edit around the real photo.** Use an AI image editor that changes the background and lighting while keeping your uploaded product. Then compare the product side by side with your original.
2. **Composite.** Cut the product out of your real photo, generate an empty scene, and place the real cutout into it in an editor. The product pixels are 100% yours. This is the bulletproof method for anything with a label.

Rule of thumb: text on the product? Composite. Plain product? Editing is usually fine, if you check it.

## Step 1: Shoot one clean source photo

Everything in this chapter starts from this one photo. Ten good minutes here saves you two hours of fixing later.

### What you need

- Your phone (any phone from the last few years is fine)
- A window with daylight, not direct sun
- A sheet of white paper or poster board, bent into a curve (a "sweep") so there's no visible corner
- A second white sheet or a piece of foam board to bounce light into the shadow side
- Optional: a stack of books to rest the phone on

### How to shoot it

1. **Set up next to the window.** Put the sweep on a table so the window is to one side of the product, not behind it.
2. **Bounce the shadows.** Hold or prop the second white sheet on the opposite side. Watch the dark side of the product brighten. That's your "fill light."
3. **Turn off overhead lights.** Mixed light (warm bulbs + cool daylight) makes colors lie.
4. **Use the main camera lens (1x) or 2x, never the ultra-wide.** Wide lenses stretch products. Step back and use 2x if you can; it flattens distortion and makes shapes look true.
5. **Shoot at product height.** For a mug or bottle, the lens should be roughly level with the middle of the product. Shooting from above makes bottles look like cones.
6. **Tap to focus on the label or the front.** Then slide exposure down slightly so whites aren't blown out.
7. **Take four angles:** straight front, three-quarter (turned about 45 degrees), side or back, and one close detail (texture, stitching, the pump, the glaze).
8. **Clean the product first.** Dust, fingerprints and lint are invisible on your table and obvious at full resolution.

> **Pro tip:** Shoot a color reference in the first frame: put a plain white sheet and something you know the exact color of next to the product. Later, if AI shifts your colors, you have proof of what "correct" looks like.

Before moving on, zoom to 100%: is the label readable, the color true, the edge clean? If not, reshoot now. AI multiplies whatever you give it, including blur.

## Step 2: White-background catalog shots

Marketplaces love a pure white background because it makes every listing look consistent. You have two routes.

### Route A: Background remover (fastest, most accurate)

Background removers don't redraw your product; they just cut it out. That's why they're my first choice for catalog work.

As of late 2026, **Adobe Express** offers a free background remover (no credit card) that handles JPG, PNG and WebP. **Canva** has one too, with limited free use and unlimited use on its paid plan. Check current pricing on both; free tiers change.

1. Upload your front, three-quarter and detail shots.
2. Remove the background.
3. Place each cutout on a pure white canvas (hex `#FFFFFF`).
4. Leave breathing room: the product should fill roughly 80–85% of the frame.
5. Add a soft shadow under the product if the editor offers it. A floating product looks pasted.

### Route B: AI edit to a studio backdrop

When your source photo has awkward light, an AI image editor can relight it and place it in a clean studio look. As of late 2026, Google's **Nano Banana Pro** (inside the Gemini app, with a limited free quota) is strong at editing an uploaded photo and handling multiple reference images. Always compare the product against your original afterwards.

```prompt
Edit this photo. Keep the [PRODUCT, e.g. ceramic coffee mug] exactly as it is: same shape, same proportions, same color, same surface finish, same handle, same details. Do not redraw, restyle or add anything to the product.

Replace only the background with a seamless pure white studio backdrop (#FFFFFF). Soft, even studio lighting from the front-left, a gentle natural contact shadow directly under the product. Product centered, filling about 80% of the frame, shot at product height with a 70mm lens look. Square 1:1 format, sharp focus, true-to-life color, no props, no text, no reflections on the backdrop.
```

> **Fix it:** If the editor changes your product's color, add the color by name and hex code: "The mug glaze is sage green (#9CAF88) — keep this exact color." If it still drifts, switch to Route A.

### The catalog set

Most stores want more than one photo. Build a consistent set from your four source angles:

| Shot | Purpose | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| Front | Main listing image | Product fills ~85% of the frame |
| Three-quarter | Shows depth and form | Same lighting direction as the front |
| Detail | Texture, material, quality | Crop tight; this is where trust is built |
| Scale | Shows real size | Your own hand holding it reads as honest |

```prompt
Edit this photo of a [PRODUCT, e.g. amber glass jar candle]. Keep the product completely unchanged: identical jar shape, glass color, wax color, lid and label. Do not alter or rewrite any text on the label.

Create a clean e-commerce detail shot: white seamless background, soft diffused light from above-left, shallow depth of field focused on [DETAIL, e.g. the wax surface and the rim of the jar]. Macro lens look, crisp texture, true color, no props, no added text. Format [1:1 or 4:5].
```

## Step 3: Styled lifestyle scenes

Lifestyle photos sell the feeling: the mug on a slow Sunday, the serum on a bathroom shelf at golden hour. This is where AI saves you a fortune in props and locations.

The secret to scenes that don't look AI: **specific, slightly imperfect, physically plausible.** A real table has a coffee ring. Real light comes from one window. Real props are a little out of place.

### The scene formula

Write every lifestyle prompt with these five parts:

1. **Keep line** — what must not change (the product)
2. **Surface** — what it sits on (linen, oak, travertine, a tiled sink edge)
3. **Setting** — the room or place, in a few concrete words
4. **Light** — one clear source with direction and time of day
5. **Props and camera** — two or three supporting objects, lens and angle

### Three ready-to-use scenes

```prompt
Edit this photo. Keep the [PRODUCT, e.g. matte sage-green ceramic coffee mug] exactly as it is: same shape, size, color, glaze finish and handle. Do not redraw or restyle it.

Place it on a weathered light-oak kitchen table near a window on a slow weekend morning. Soft side light from a window on the left, warm early sun, gentle long shadows. Props: a folded off-white linen napkin, a half-eaten slice of toast on a small plate, a few coffee beans scattered loosely. Slight steam rising from the mug. Shot at table height with a 50mm lens, shallow depth of field, background softly blurred. Natural colors, subtle film grain, realistic and lived-in, not overly tidy. Format 4:5.
```

```prompt
Edit this photo. Keep the [PRODUCT, e.g. frosted glass skincare dropper bottle] exactly as it is: identical bottle shape, glass finish, dropper, cap color and label. Do not change, blur or rewrite any text on the label.

Place it on the edge of a white ceramic bathroom sink with small water droplets on the surface. Morning light from a frosted window on the right, soft and bright. Props: a folded cotton face towel, a small green plant leaf at the edge of the frame. Clean but real: a few water spots, not sterile. 85mm lens look, eye level with the bottle, background a soft blur of pale tiles. True color, photorealistic, no added text. Format 4:5.
```

```prompt
Edit this photo. Keep the [PRODUCT, e.g. plain white low-top sneakers with no logos] exactly as they are: same shape, sole, stitching, laces and color. Do not add any logos, stripes or text.

Place them on a sunlit concrete step outside a city apartment building in late afternoon. Warm low sun from behind-left creating a soft rim light on the sneakers and long shadows. Props: a corner of a denim jacket draped on the step, a takeaway coffee cup slightly out of focus. Shot from a low angle at step height with a 35mm lens, natural street texture, gentle film grain, realistic, unposed feel. Format 4:5.
```

> **Fix it:** If the product looks "pasted in," the light doesn't match. Name the same light direction for the shadows: "the mug casts a soft shadow toward the right, matching the window light from the left."

> **Pro tip:** If your label still comes out garbled after two tries, stop fighting the generator. Generate the scene with a plain stand-in object (or with the product removed), then composite your real cutout into it in an editor and paint a soft shadow underneath. Ten minutes, zero label risk.

### The composite route, step by step

1. Generate an empty scene with the prompt below.
2. Cut out your product with a background remover.
3. Drop the cutout into the scene in your editor and scale it to match nearby props (a mug is about the size of a fist).
4. Add a soft, slightly transparent shadow on the side away from the light, then compare the product to your original.

```prompt
A photorealistic empty styled scene for product photography: a [SURFACE, e.g. pale travertine stone shelf] in a [SETTING, e.g. calm minimalist bathroom]. Clear empty space in the center foreground where a product will be placed later. No product, no bottles, no text.

Lighting: [LIGHT, e.g. soft morning daylight from a window on the left]. Props pushed to the edges: [PROPS, e.g. a rolled white towel on the right, a small eucalyptus sprig on the left]. Camera at shelf height, 85mm lens look, shallow depth of field, background softly blurred. Natural colors, subtle grain, realistic. Format [4:5 or 1:1].
```

## Step 4: Seasonal sets

Same product, new season: that's how small shops keep their feed and ads fresh without new stock or new shoots.

Pick a season and change only three things: **surface, props, light color.** Keep camera angle and framing the same so the set looks like a planned campaign.

| Season | Surface | Props | Light |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autumn | Dark walnut, wool throw | Dried leaves, cinnamon sticks, a small pumpkin | Warm, low, amber afternoon |
| Holiday / winter | Knit blanket, frosted window ledge | Pine sprigs, warm string lights out of focus, wrapped box with plain paper | Cozy tungsten glow, deep shadows |
| Spring | Pale linen, light wood | Fresh tulips, a small ceramic dish | Bright, soft, cool morning |
| Summer | Sun-bleached stone, poolside tile | Citrus slices, a straw hat edge, water ripples in shadow | Hard midday sun, crisp shadows |

```prompt
Edit this photo. Keep the [PRODUCT, e.g. amber glass jar candle with a cream label] exactly as it is: identical jar, wax, lid, colors and label. Do not change or rewrite any label text.

Restyle the scene for [SEASON, e.g. a cozy winter holiday evening]. Surface: [SURFACE, e.g. a chunky cream knit blanket on a wooden side table]. Props: [PROPS, e.g. a few pine sprigs, a small gift wrapped in plain brown paper with twine, warm string lights glowing out of focus in the background]. Light: [LIGHT, e.g. warm candlelight glow and soft tungsten light from the right, deep cozy shadows]. The candle is lit with a small natural flame.

Same camera as a standard product shot: eye level, 50mm lens look, product centered, shallow depth of field. Photorealistic, subtle film grain, no text. Format [4:5].
```

> **Heads up:** Keep holiday props generic. Plain paper, pine, lights and ribbons work everywhere. Avoid famous characters, branded wrapping or anything that looks like another company's packaging.

## Step 5: The ad grid — 9 variations from one photo

Ads need variety. Not because one photo is bad, but because you don't know yet which background, mood or angle your audience will stop for. A 3×3 grid gives you nine distinct ideas from one product photo, so you can test them side by side.

Plan the grid on purpose. Change **one main variable per row** so you learn something:

| | Column 1 | Column 2 | Column 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Row 1: Background color** | Warm terracotta | Soft sage | Deep navy |
| **Row 2: Setting** | Kitchen counter | Desk by a window | Outdoor café table |
| **Row 3: Angle and crop** | Top-down flat lay | Tight close-up detail | Hand holding the product |

### Generating the grid

Some tools can produce a grid in one image, but each cell comes out small and the product is more likely to drift. I generate each cell separately with the same keep line, then assemble the grid in an editor. More work, much better accuracy.

Use this template nine times, changing only the bracketed variable:

```prompt
Edit this photo. Keep the [PRODUCT, e.g. matte white ceramic mug] exactly as it is: same shape, proportions, color, finish and handle. Do not add text, logos or patterns to the product.

Variation: [VARIABLE, e.g. a solid warm terracotta seamless background with a soft gradient / a bright desk by a window with a notebook and pen / a top-down flat lay on linen with coffee beans].

Lighting: soft, directional, from the left, with a natural shadow that matches the scene. Clean, modern, premium advertising photography. Photorealistic, true color, sharp product, no text, no watermark. Format 1:1.
```

For the "hand holding" cell, use your own hand from your source photos, or a hand that clearly isn't a recognizable person.

```prompt
Edit this photo. Keep the [PRODUCT, e.g. frosted glass skincare dropper bottle] exactly as it is, including the label text and the dropper shape. Do not rewrite or blur the label.

Show the product held naturally in a relaxed adult hand with neat, unpolished short nails and natural skin texture with visible fine lines and pores. The hand enters from the bottom right of the frame. Soft daylight from a window on the left, warm neutral background softly out of focus. 85mm lens look, shallow depth of field, photorealistic, true color, no text. Format 4:5.
```

> **Fix it:** Hands are the classic AI tell. Count the fingers, check the thumb bends the right way, and look for fingers melting into the bottle. If it's off, regenerate or use a photo of your real hand.

### Assemble and review

Lay all nine out in a 3×3 grid. Zoom out: the three that make you look twice go into your first ad test. Zoom in: delete any cell where the product drifted. Accuracy beats variety.

## Step 6: Text and logos go in the editor, not the generator

This is the one habit that separates "AI-looking" product ads from professional ones.

Generators have gotten much better at text (Nano Banana Pro is noticeably good at it as of late 2026), but "better" isn't "reliable." One wrong letter in a price or a product name, and the ad is unusable. And a logo regenerated by AI is never quite your logo.

So:

1. **Generate the image with no text at all.** End every prompt with "no text, no watermark."
2. **Leave space on purpose.** Ask for "clean empty space in the top third for a headline" when you plan a text-heavy ad.
3. **Add headlines, prices, badges and your logo in an editor** (Canva, Adobe Express or any layout tool) as real, editable text and your real logo file.
4. **Keep it short.** One headline, one supporting line, one call to action. The image does the rest.

```prompt
Edit this photo. Keep the [PRODUCT, e.g. plain white low-top sneakers with no logos] exactly as they are; do not add any branding.

Place the sneakers in the lower right of the frame on a smooth [COLOR, e.g. pale butter-yellow] seamless studio background. Leave a large clean empty area across the top half of the frame for a headline to be added later. Soft studio light from the upper left, crisp natural shadow to the lower right. Minimal, premium ad photography, photorealistic, true color. No text, no letters, no watermark. Format [9:16].
```

## Step 7: Formats for marketplaces, Instagram and ads

Generate at the format you'll use, or crop with room to spare. A product cropped too tight at the edges can't be saved later.

| Where | Ratio | Common size (px) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marketplace main image | 1:1 | 2000 × 2000 | Usually white background, product fills most of the frame; check each marketplace's rules |
| Instagram feed | 4:5 | 1080 × 1350 | Takes the most vertical space in the feed |
| Instagram / Facebook Stories and Reels | 9:16 | 1080 × 1920 | Keep the product and text away from the top and bottom ~250px, where buttons and captions sit |
| Feed ads (Meta) | 1:1 or 4:5 | 1080 × 1080 / 1080 × 1350 | 4:5 is a safe default for mobile |
| Online store banner | ~16:9 or wider | 1920 × 1080 or wider | Put the product off-center, leave space for text |
| Pinterest | 2:3 | 1000 × 1500 | Tall, styled scenes do well here |

Platform specs change; double-check the current recommended sizes in each platform's help center before a big batch.

### Reframing an image you already like

If you love a 1:1 scene but need 9:16 for Stories, don't regenerate from scratch. Use an outpaint or "expand" feature to extend the scene upward and downward.

```prompt
Extend this image to a [TARGET RATIO, e.g. 9:16 vertical] format. Do not change anything that is already in the image: keep the [PRODUCT], the surface, the props and the lighting exactly as they are. Continue the scene naturally above and below: more of the [SETTING, e.g. light-oak table and soft blurred kitchen wall above, the table surface below]. Match the light direction, color temperature, grain and depth of field of the original. No new products, no text.
```

> **Ethics:** If you use AI scenes in ads, follow each platform's AI disclosure rules (for example, Meta's "AI info" label, TikTok's AI-generated content label, and YouTube's altered or synthetic content disclosure). And be honest in what you show: an AI-styled scene is fine; an AI-improved product (shinier, bigger, a color you don't sell) is misleading.

## Your final accuracy check

Before any image goes live, put it next to your source photo and run this 60-second check:

1. **Shape:** Same proportions? Overlay them if you're unsure.
2. **Color:** Same hue in the same light? Check against your color reference shot.
3. **Label:** Every word spelled right, same font, same position?
4. **Material:** Matte still matte, glass still glass?
5. **Details:** Handles, lids, stitching, pumps all present?
6. **Scale:** Does the product look its real size next to the props?

If any answer is no, fix it or don't use it. A gorgeous photo of the wrong product is a returns problem.

## Checklist

- [ ] I shot a clean source photo in window light, at product height, on the 1x or 2x lens
- [ ] I have four angles plus a detail shot, all sharp at 100% zoom
- [ ] My catalog shots use a background remover or an edit I compared against the original
- [ ] Every prompt includes a "keep the product exactly as it is" line
- [ ] Any product with a label was checked letter by letter, or composited from the real photo
- [ ] All headlines, prices and logos were added in an editor, not generated
- [ ] I exported each image in the right ratio for where it will be used
- [ ] I know the AI disclosure rules for the platforms I post on

## Try it now

**15 minutes: one product, three photos.**

1. (5 min) Grab any product near you — a mug, a candle, a bottle. Set it by a window on white paper and shoot a front and a three-quarter angle at product height.
2. (3 min) Remove the background with a free remover and place the front shot on pure white. That's your catalog photo.
3. (5 min) Run the morning-kitchen lifestyle prompt from Step 3 on the three-quarter shot, swapping in your product's description and color.
4. (2 min) Put the result next to your original. Check shape, color and any text. If it passes, you just did a product shoot without a studio.
